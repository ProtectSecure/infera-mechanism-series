The Terms

Episode 1 followed the paper; Episode 2 followed the room. This one follows the split: the deals through which Black artists and executives rewrote who gets what, from half a cent a record to eighty-five fifteen to one hundred percent, and what each of them had to give up to get it.

Hero: a generative circuit drawn in code, one segment cut. No photograph, no real place.

AS OF Oct 30, 2026 READ ~45 min whole · 6 min per exhibit LAYER Public record · SEC filings · court complaints · contracts as published · trade press METHOD SVNIF

A deal memo is the shortest document in the business and the one that decides everything after it: the advance, the rate, who holds the masters, who holds the publishing, what the label takes off the top, and what happens when the term ends. For most of the century the memo was written for the artist. This episode is about the ones that were written by, or rewritten for, the people whose work it covered.

Where the record gives the terms, the page prints them exactly: 60/40 after recoupment, $2.6 billion cash and $400 million in stock, 50.01 against 49.99. Where the record gives only an artist's own account, a litigant's claim, or a trade estimate, the figure wears a dashed chip and the page says whose number it is. Several famous splits exist in two versions; both are shown. No figure in this episode is a measurement of what an artist is worth. Every one is a measurement of what a piece of paper said.

READING MEMO · Every exhibit stands alone. Fact is on the record, Reported is a credible account we could not independently confirm, Inference is our arithmetic, Watch is open as of the dateline. Disputed marks a term the record gives in more than one version. The engine's flow levels are this page's reading, anchored to the figure on each card. This is an original Infera investigation from the public record; for the long history of Black creative ownership it runs alongside, see Lisa E. Davis, Our Minds Were Always Free (Simon & Schuster, 2026), listed in the sources as a further read.77
The spine

Three findings, stated first

The exhibits are the evidence. These are the verdicts.

  1. 1971 to 2003FactCapital, not price, was the gate. Inner City Broadcasting needed $1.1 million to buy WBLS in 1974 and was turned down by thirty-six banks. Sugar Hill's seed money came from Morris Levy. The first independents to keep their masters did it with distribution deals, not loans, and two of the three went bankrupt anyway.2, 8, 18
  2. 1995 to 2020FactThe split moved; the structure did not, until it was posted. TLC filed for bankruptcy on a seven percent rate while selling ten million; Megan Thee Stallion's 2018 contract gave the label 60 percent after recoupment, half the copyrights and 30 percent of everything else; Kanye West's posted pages show one artist's arc from a 14 percent royalty to a profit split to "100 percent minus a distribution fee."27, 52, 54
  3. 2007 to 2026FactEquity beat royalty, and the biggest equity was outside music. Beats sold for $3 billion; Fenty is 49.99 percent of a brand worth about $2.8 billion; the Cîroc "equal share" ended with the artist exiting both brands; the AI settlements of 2025 pay the opt-in and nothing to the opt-out.31, 34, 67

The Handshake

Instrument: the bank's answer

The first Black-owned terms were written by people who could find the deal but not the money. Inner City Broadcasting, founded in 1970 by Percy Sutton and Clarence Jones with more than fifty shareholders, bought WLIB in 1972, reportedly for $1.9 million; in 1974 it needed $1.1 million to exercise its right to buy the FM that became WBLS and was turned down by thirty-six banks.1, 2

FactSutton bought the shuttered Apollo for $250,000 in 1981. Creditors forced Inner City into bankruptcy in August 2011; its stations went to a Burkle-Magic Johnson vehicle for a reported $180 million in 2012 and WBLS and WLIB to Emmis for $131 million in 2014. NABOB said none of the seventeen former Inner City stations went to a Black buyer.3, 4

FactSugar Hill Records was founded in 1979 by Sylvia and Joe Robinson and funded by Tony Riviera and Morris Levy of Roulette. "Rapper's Delight" is certified at about five million worldwide and reported at fourteen. Chic's writers settled for credits; Grandmaster Caz, who wrote the verses, got none.5, 6

FactIn 1983 Sugar Hill signed with MCA through a Levy associate; by 1985 MCA had taken the Chess catalog against $3 million in debt; the Robinsons sued for $240 million, said they had received $481,000, and settled in 1990 keeping Sugar Hill and losing Chess.7 InferenceA Black-owned label lost a Black catalog to a major in a debt workout, the exit from Episode 2 run from the inside.

FactDon Robey took writer credit as "Deadric Malone" on Duke/Peacock songs, paying, by one of his artists' accounts, "25 or 50 bucks" for them. Clarence Avant's Sussex Records was seized by the IRS in 1975 over $48,000 in liens; Bill Withers said of it, "They weren't paying me."8, 9 InferenceOwnership changed who held the paper. It did not, by itself, change what the paper said.

FactJune 28, 1988: Motown sold to MCA and Boston Ventures for $61 million, Jobete publishing, the Hitsville studio and the plants excluded; Gordy insisted that 20 percent of the equity be available to minority investors. The story that Avant brokered it rests on obituaries and a 2019 documentary, not on the trade press of the day.10, 11

Eighty-Five Fifteen

Instrument: the distribution deal

Two kinds of deal built the 1990s. One was financed: Interscope put $10 million into Death Row in 1992 to finance and distribute it, and the label peaked above $100 million a year, lost Dre in 1996, lost its distributor in 1997 and was auctioned out of bankruptcy for $18 million in 2009. The other was distributed: the label kept the masters and paid a fee for the trucks.12

ReportedMaster P's No Limit signed with Priority in 1996 and kept its masters and its studio; by his own 2021 account the split was 85 to No Limit and 15 to Priority with a $375,000 advance per album, and he had turned down $1 million from Interscope. In 1998 the label put out 23 albums, 15 million units, and, he said, more than $150 million; Forbes put his personal take at $56.5 million. No Limit filed for bankruptcy in December 2003.13, 14

ReportedCash Money's March 1998 Universal deal was estimated at $30 million over three years: a $2 million annual advance, $1.5 million per artist for up to six a year, masters and publishing retained. The split is in the record two ways, Universal 20 and Cash Money 80, or 15 and 85 after recoupment. Disputed Birdman has said the masters still earn $20 to $30 million a year.15, 16

FactInside the masters-owning label the artist still fought. Lil Wayne sued Cash Money for $51 million in January 2015 over Tha Carter V; the parties settled in 2018 and Young Money passed to him outright; his former manager's 2020 complaint alleges he then sold the Young Money masters to Universal for more than $100 million. Universal declined comment.17

ReportedRoc-A-Fella sold half of itself to Def Jam in June 1997 for an estimated $1.4 million, a year after Reasonable Doubt; in December 2004 Island Def Jam bought the other half for $10 million and made Jay-Z its president. The masters sit in a three-way-owned corporation, not with him personally. So So Def's 1992 Sony deal capped Jermaine Dupri's producer royalty at 8 percent, then 6; he sued Sony for $18 million in July 2026 and ended the suit in August.18, 19, 20

Platinum and Bankrupt

Instrument: the royalty statement

The baseline the later deals were rewriting: Little Richard's 1955 Specialty contract paid half a cent a record and $50 for the publishing on "Tutti Frutti"; he got $25,000 from the first 500,000. In 1984 he sued for $112 million and settled for an amount never disclosed.22, 23

FactRuth Brown left Atlantic in 1960 owing $30,000 in charged expenses; the label's own accounting said she had earned $754 in royalties between 1960 and 1980 while $25,000 in debt, at a rate of 4.5 percent. In 1988, approaching its fortieth anniversary and with the lawyer Howell Begle pressing, Atlantic waived the unrecouped debts of its R&B artists, recalculated royalties back to 1970, and reset her rate to 10 percent. Her first check in 28 years was $20,000 to $30,000; the label seeded the Rhythm & Blues Foundation with $1.5 to $2 million.24, 25

InferenceThe first label-wide retroactive royalty reform in the record was won without a lawsuit, and most other majors followed by precedent. It is the reform the next two cases show was not enough.

ReportedTLC's 1991 contract through Pebbitone and LaFace paid about 7 percent of each album; after expenses, about 20 cents, split three ways. On July 3, 1995, with CrazySexyCool on its way to fourteen million, the group filed for Chapter 11 with $3.5 million in debts, each member clearing under $50,000 a year. The 1996 settlement cut out the production company; the terms were confidential.26, 27

ReportedToni Braxton sued LaFace in December 1997 and filed for bankruptcy in January 1998; her lawyer's account put her contract at 33 cents an album on more than twenty-four million sold. Her memoir reports a $20 million settlement and a ten-year gag order; no filing confirms the number.28, 29 InferenceTwo Arista/LaFace acts used bankruptcy as contract leverage within thirty months of each other. The recoupable advance from Episode 1, as a court file.

The Equity Turn

Instrument: the cap table

A royalty is a share of a sale. Equity is a share of the company that makes the sale. The deals in this chapter are the ones where a Black artist got the second kind, and the record shows that the biggest of them were not in music.30

FactCîroc, 2007: an "equal-share venture," profits split between Combs and Diageo, on a brand that had struggled to sell 40,000 cases; nearly two million a year by 2014. DeLeón, December 2013: a true 50/50. In May 2023 Combs sued Diageo over its investment in the brands; Diageo said he sought "to extract additional billions"; in January 2024 the suit was dropped and Diageo became sole owner of both. No payment was disclosed.30, 31

FactBeats: HTC bought a majority for about $300 million in 2011 and sold it back by 2013; Carlyle put in $500 million at more than $1 billion. On May 28, 2014 Apple bought the company for $3 billion, $2.6 billion in cash and $400 million in stock vesting over time. Forbes put Dre's stake at 20 to 25 percent; the after-tax figure is a derived range, never disclosed.32, 33

FactTidal: bought for $56 million in March 2015; Sprint took a third for $200 million in January 2017; Square's 8-K records a majority purchase closing April 30, 2021 for approximately $302 million, with Carter and affiliates retaining a minority interest, percentage undisclosed. Fenty Beauty, 2017: LVMH 50.01 percent, Rihanna 49.99, €500 million in its first full year; Forbes values the brand near $2.8 billion.34, 35, 36

ReportedSpringHill sold a minority stake at a $725 million valuation in October 2021; HartBeat sold 15 percent to Abry for $100 million in April 2022; Westbrook sold about 10 percent to Candle Media at about $600 million in January 2022, and by 2024 had cut half its staff with revenue reported down from $170 million to $100 million. Adidas paid Yeezy an 11 percent royalty, about $191 million in 2020, and terminated it in October 2022; the 2024 settlement moved no money.37, 38, 39, 40

FactNipsey Hussle's Crenshaw, October 2013: a free download, and a thousand physical copies at $100 each that brought about $100,000 in under a day; Jay-Z bought a hundred. His 2017 Atlantic partnership's terms were never published.41, 42 InferenceOne hundred percent of a $100 unit against cents on an album: the smallest deal in the chapter and the cleanest.

The Clause War

Instrument: the contract, read aloud

In September 1992 Prince signed what the press called a $100 million deal with Warner Bros.: six albums at $10 million each, a vice-president's title, financing for his label. It did not include his masters. By 1993 he had written "slave" on his face; by 1996 he had left. On April 18, 2014 Warner agreed that he "will take ownership of the master recordings of his previous Warner Bros. releases." Twenty-two years.43, 44, 45

FactGeorge Clinton's publishing passed to Armen Boladian's Bridgeport Music in the 1970s on assignments Clinton calls forged; in 2001 Bridgeport filed more than 500 infringement counts against more than 800 artists and labels, and in 2005 won the Sixth Circuit's bright line, "Get a license or do not sample." In 2013 a court assigned four Clinton copyrights to his own former lawyers to satisfy a $1.5 million fee; in September 2025 a Michigan judge dismissed the Worrell estate's co-ownership claims, and Clinton's own suit against Boladian is pending.46, 47, 48

FactDe La Soul's six albums, 1989 to 2001, were off streaming for 27 years over sample clearances in the Tommy Boy contracts; Reservoir bought Tommy Boy for about $100 million in 2021 and said "the first call we made was to De La Soul." The catalog went live on March 3, 2023.49

FactMegan Thee Stallion's February 2018 contract with 1501 Certified, read by Rolling Stone: a $10,000 advance; 60 percent to the label and 40 to the artist after recoupment, recording costs off the top; an undivided 50 percent of her copyrights to the label's publisher; 30 percent of merchandise, sponsorships, live income and outside features over $1,000, and of "all entertainment-related endeavors." She sued in March 2020 and settled in October 2023, terms undisclosed.50, 51

FactOn September 16, 2020 Kanye West posted pages of his contracts: a 2005 agreement with a $2.8 million advance and a royalty from 14 percent rising to 18; 2012 amendments moving albums six and seven to a 50 percent profit split and albums eight and nine to "100 percent minus a distribution fee," with masters for albums six to ten owned or reverting. Kelis said in January 2020 she had been promised a 33/33/33 publishing split with the Neptunes "which we didn't do," and made nothing from her first two albums.52, 53

ReportedFrank Ocean delivered Endless to Def Jam on August 19, 2016 and released Blonde on his own label the next day; Billboard's sources put his profit share going from 14 to 70 percent. The Recording Academy made streaming-only releases eligible in June 2016 and Chance the Rapper won three Grammys with no label; Apple, he said, "gave me half a mil and a commercial." Lauryn Hill settled the New Ark credit suit for a reported $5 million. Taylor Swift's masters were sold twice over her objection and bought back in May 2025 for a reported $300 to $360 million: the contrast case.54, 55, 56, 57

The Lot

Instrument: the deed to the land

Episode 1 called it the Harpo switch: own the show, not the slot. This chapter is what the switch cost and what it was worth when the people who threw it sold. The room, as a capital expenditure.58

FactJune 2015: Atlanta's redevelopment authority approved selling 330 of Fort McPherson's 488 acres to Tyler Perry for $30 million; the authority had paid $26 million for the whole base. March 13, 2026: Paramount bought Perry's equity stake in BET+, believed to be a quarter, for "tens of millions," as it folded the service into Paramount+; his nine-figure overall deal with BET runs to 2028.58, 59

FactOWN launched on January 1, 2011 as a 50/50 Discovery-Harpo joint venture on Discovery's $100 million; in December 2017 Discovery bought another 24.5 percent for $70 million, pricing the network near $280 million; in December 2020 Winfrey sold most of the rest for about $36 million in Discovery stock and extended her exclusivity through 2025.60, 61 InferenceAbout $106 million across the two sales for a network built on her name: a good exit, a modest one, and a full one.

ReportedByron Allen bought the Weather Channel's cable network for about $300 million in March 2018 and more than $1 billion of television stations after it; on August 8, 2025 he sold ten of them to Gray Media for $171 million to cut debt. Paramount shelved the BET sale in 2023 at his $3.5 billion bid.62, 63

ReportedThe overall deal is the lot's opposite: Shonda Rhimes's Netflix pact, reported at $100 to $150 million in 2017 and $300 to $400 million at renewal in 2021; Ava DuVernay's $100 million Warner deal, which ended at term in 2023; Issa Rae's WarnerMedia deal, estimated at $40 million, which she disputed in public, "any number you read is cap"; Jordan Peele's five-year Universal partnership, exclusive but not equity.64, 65, 66

One Hundred Percent

Instrument: the opt-in

UnitedMasters launched in November 2017 on a $70 million round led by Alphabet. From July 2020 it kept 10 percent of royalties on its standard tier and nothing on a $5-a-month tier: "artists maintain 100 percent ownership of their careers." In March 2021 Apple led a $50 million round at a reported $550 million valuation. By 2026 ownership is a $20-to-$60-a-year subscription at any of three distributors.67, 68, 69

ReportedWhat one hundred percent sells for, when it is sold: Dr. Dre's income streams from two solo albums, N.W.A and his production and writing to Shamrock and Universal for upwards of $200 million in January 2023; Lil Wayne's Young Money masters for more than $100 million by his former manager's complaint. Against them: Bob Dylan's songs for about $300 million, Springsteen's catalog for about $500 million, Timberlake's for more than $100 million, and Blackstone's $1.6 billion for Hipgnosis's 65,000 tracks.70, 17, 71, 72

InferenceThe Black catalog sales of 2020 to 2023 are younger assets sold on private terms, and the gap with the rock deals is partly age and partly the terms nobody can see. What the record does show is that the highest number in the chapter belongs to a fund, not an artist.

FactOctober 29, 2025: Universal settled with Udio for a compensatory payment and licenses; a walled-garden platform follows in 2026. November 25, 2025: Warner settled with Suno, with "an opt-in for the use of their name, image, likeness, voice and compositions." Reporting on the Universal deal describes the structure: the artist who opts in is paid for training and for subscribers' creations; the artist who opts out gets nothing. No dollar figure in either settlement is public.73, 74, 75

WatchThe NO FAKES Act, reintroduced in May and June 2026, would create a federal, licensable right in voice and likeness with notice-and-takedown; the Senate Judiciary Committee advanced it unanimously on June 23, 2026. Not enacted as of the dateline. It would make the thing the AI license covers a tradeable deed, and the question of who holds it is the one this episode ends on.76

Closing

Three findings, one direction

What the record supports, and what it does not.

Capital was the gate: the handshake got the deed and thirty-six banks decided whether it could be held. The split moved, from half a cent to 85/15 to 100 percent, and the structure did not move until an artist posted his pages. Equity beat royalty, and the biggest equity was a pair of headphones and a beauty line. What is open is the newest term of all: an opt-in, in a license between a label and a machine, for the use of a voice.

What the record supports

  • The Square 8-K, the Sixth Circuit opinion, the 1501 contract as published, Kanye West's posted pages, the Apple-Beats terms and the two AI settlements' public statements are primary (T1) or contemporaneous press (T2).
  • The Atlantic reform, the TLC and Braxton filings and the Motown price are dated to the trade paper of the day.
  • Every exit is tied to a sale, a court or an auction.

What it does not

  • The No Limit 85/15 and $375,000 advance are Master P's own account; Cash Money's split is in the record as both 80/20 and 85/15; the Young Money sale is a litigant's claim.
  • Dre's Beats take, Carter's retained Tidal stake, the Braxton settlement, the Ocean share and every overall-deal value are estimates or memoir, and are chipped as such.
  • Clarence Avant as the broker of the Motown sale rests on obituaries and a documentary; the trade press of July 1988 names no advisor.
