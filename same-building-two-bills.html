Are data centers raising your electric bill?

Two serious bodies of evidence give opposite answers. This exhibit shows that both can be right at once, and lets you find the conditions under which each one holds.

Both numbers are real. They come from different years, different places and different mechanisms. The rest of this page takes them apart.

EXHIBIT A · THE AUCTION

The price of keeping the lights on rose more than ninefold in one auction

PJM runs the grid for 13 states and Washington, DC. Every year it holds an auction that pays power plants to be available on the hottest and coldest days. The price is set in dollars per megawatt per day. Tap a year for its price.

The 2025/26 auction cleared at $269.92, up from $28.92 the year before. Every auction since has hit the price cap set with the 13 governors and FERC. FACT
RTO clearing price, $/MW-day UCAP. 2025/26 cleared higher in two zones: $466.35 in BGE (Maryland) and $444.26 in Dominion (Virginia). Sources: PJM, Citizens Utility Board, IEEFA.
EXHIBIT B · WHO GETS THE BLAME

Every driver can claim a big share, and the claims add up to more than the bill

The 2025/26 auction cost PJM customers $14.7 billion, up from $2.2 billion. Two expert analyses each asked what the bill would have been without one driver. Switch each driver on to stack its claimed share against the actual total.

Data center figure: IMM, load above and within the embedded forecast, $9.33 billion. Rule change ($4.4 billion, revenue 49.1% higher) and reliability-must-run treatment of Brandon Shores and Wagner ($4.3 billion, 41.2% higher): Synapse for the DC Office of People's Counsel, April 2025. Each uses its own counterfactual, so they cannot be added. That is the point of the stack.

INFERENCE When drivers interact, a scarce grid makes each one more expensive than it would be alone. Each side can then credibly blame the driver it prefers. The useful question is which drivers policy can change, not which one is "the" cause.

BOTH SIDES, AT THEIR STRONGEST

The best argument each camp makes

Illustration from the Infera image archive, made with Midjourney. Not a photograph of a real facility or home.

Data centers are not the reason bills are rising

  • Big new customers spread fixed wire costs over more kilowatt-hours. Two national studies find lower rates where load grew. REPORTED
  • Nominal residential prices rose 33% from 2019 to 2025, driven mostly by distribution, transmission, storm and wildfire costs and gas prices. FACT
  • In PJM, market rule changes and reliability decisions each moved the 2025/26 auction by billions on their own. REPORTED
  • Voters who blame data centers rose from 28% to 53% in a year (Heatmap Pro), faster than the evidence moved. REPORTED

Data centers are already on your bill

  • PJM's own market monitor calls data center demand "the primary reason" for the capacity price spike. REPORTED
  • The monitor puts the added cost at $23.1 billion over three auctions, borne by other customers. FACT
  • The national studies end in 2024, before the price shock reached homes in June 2025 and before the largest campuses came online. FACT
  • Forecast load that is never built still raised the price: removing forecast data center load alone cut 2027/28 auction revenue by $6.24 billion. FACT

The fight crosses party lines. The Ratepayer Protection Act, pushed by Sen. Jon Husted (R-Ohio), passed the House 417 to 3. Sen. Martin Heinrich (D-N.M.) blocked fast Senate passage because the bill relies on voluntary state commitments, and he wants operators required to pay their energy costs REPORTED. Candidates in both parties are running against data centers this fall REPORTED.

WHAT THIS EXHIBIT DID NOT PROVE

We did not prove that data centers raised or lowered any specific household's bill. The capacity figures describe PJM, which covers about a fifth of the country. Hedged load does not pay auction prices directly, so the auction total is not the same as what customers paid. Exhibit C's split is our arithmetic on two published figures. This page leaves out water, land, local tax revenue and jobs, which drive much of the opposition; The Data Center Bill covers the tax side for Georgia.

What would change this read: household-level studies covering 2025 and 2026; utility filings that split capacity costs by cause; large-load tariffs that show whether data centers end up paying their own grid costs.

FORM YOUR OWN READ

Four questions to ask about your own utility

  1. Is your utility in a capacity market like PJM, or does it own its plants and set rates through a state commission?
  2. Does your state have a large-load tariff that makes data centers pay for the substations and lines built for them?
  3. How much of your utility's forecast growth is data centers that have signed contracts, as opposed to requests in the queue?
  4. When your rates last changed, what did the filing list as the causes, and in what proportions?
