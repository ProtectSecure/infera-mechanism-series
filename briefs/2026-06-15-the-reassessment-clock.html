North Carolina law requires every county to reassess every eight years.
Pennsylvania law requires nothing.

Allegheny County, Pennsylvania last reassessed property values on May 18, 2012. That was 5,142 days ago. In Mecklenburg County, North Carolina, the most recent reassessment was 2023, on a four-year cycle. When a county lets the years pile up between reassessments, the unfairness compounds: fast-appreciating neighborhoods underpay, slow-appreciating neighborhoods overpay, school districts get distorted revenue. The longer the wait, the more regressively wrong the bill becomes. This is a clock that runs backwards.

SCENE · TWO HOUSES, ONE COUNTY

Imagine a modest brick house on a side street in Penn Hills, on the east side of Pittsburgh. It was last assessed in 2012 at $98,000. The neighborhood has not appreciated. The house is worth roughly $98,000 today. The owner pays property tax on $98,000.

Three miles south, in Squirrel Hill, a similar-sized house was also assessed in 2012, at $310,000. The neighborhood has appreciated. The house is worth roughly $720,000 today. The owner pays property tax on $310,000.

Both owners pay the same mill rate. Only one is paying it on what their house is actually worth.

WHAT THE COUNTER MEANS

Each day the counter ticks up, the gap between assessed values and market values gets wider — but unevenly. The properties whose neighborhoods have appreciated faster than the county average are underpaying. The properties whose neighborhoods have appreciated more slowly are overpaying. The unfairness is not stochastic. It tracks the same gradient as race, class, and neighborhood history.

EXHIBIT BDID YOU KNOW · A POP QUIZ

Three questions about who reassesses, when, and what happens when they don't. Most people guess wrong on at least two.

Property reassessment cycles vary by state, and the variation has real consequences. Take a guess on each before reading the reveal.

QUESTION 01 · STATUTORY CYCLES
How many U.S. states have no statutory requirement that counties periodically reassess property values?
About eleven. Pennsylvania, Illinois, Indiana, Texas, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, and a handful of others have no mandatory statewide reassessment cycle. The remaining states typically require reassessment every 3, 4, 6, 8, or 10 years. The states that don't require it routinely produce assessment ratios that drift by 20-50% within a single county, with the worst inequities concentrated in neighborhoods whose values diverge from the county mean. Source: International Association of Assessing Officers · 2024 statewide policy review
QUESTION 02 · WHO PAYS WHEN ASSESSMENTS GO STALE
In a county with stale assessments, which kind of property is most likely to be paying more than its fair share of property tax?
Slow-appreciating working-class neighborhoods. Counterintuitive but well-documented. When a county's assessments are stale, every property is being taxed on its old value. Homes in neighborhoods that appreciated faster than the county average end up underpaying relative to current market value. Homes in neighborhoods that appreciated more slowly end up overpaying — they got valued in a higher-multiple era and the rest of the market caught up around them. The net effect is regressive: the working-class taxpayer subsidizes the gentrifier. Source: Berry & Beresteanu, "Property Tax Misperception" (UChicago Harris)
QUESTION 03 · SCHOOL FUNDING
When a Pennsylvania county runs on stale assessments, where does the distortion most concretely land?
School district revenue. Most Pennsylvania school districts are funded largely from local property tax, with the millage rate set by the school board. When property values are stale, the district receives the same total revenue (the rate adjusts) — but the distribution of who pays it gets wrong. Wealthier districts inside a stale county tend to extract MORE revenue per pupil from each assessed dollar than neighboring poorer districts, because the wealthy districts' assessment ratios have drifted in their favor. The Pennsylvania State Tax Equalization Board (STEB) tracks this drift with its Common Level Ratio, but the CLR adjustment doesn't fix the regressive distribution within districts. Source: PA Independent Fiscal Office · school funding equity report
EXHIBIT CTWO COUNTIES · ONE QUESTION

Allegheny PA and Wake NC. Both around 1.2 million people. One has a clock, one doesn't.

The comparison isn't ideological. Both counties have engaged tax authorities, both have professional assessor's offices, both run reasonable mass-appraisal models. The difference is the statutory frame. North Carolina requires reassessment within 8 years (NCGS 105-286). Pennsylvania requires nothing.

EXHIBIT ETHE INVERSION

This is what stale assessment does over time. The bill itself becomes unjust.

Two parallel scenarios. Same county. Same total revenue collected. Same mill rate. The only variable is whether the county reassessed two years ago or fourteen years ago. The distribution flips.

SCENARIO A · FAIR · RECENT REASSESSMENT

Property tax distributed roughly in proportion to current market value.

Wealthier neighborhoods (higher current value) pay more. Working-class neighborhoods (lower current value) pay less. The distribution tracks ability to pay. The county collects the same total revenue.

ACTIONWHAT TO DO ABOUT YOUR ASSESSMENT

Unlike the other clocks, this one can be fought one parcel at a time. The system is biased toward people who appeal.

Most counties accept assessment appeals in a defined window each year, usually between issuance of the annual notice and a hard appeal deadline a few weeks later. The appeal rate is low. The win rate on appeals that are filed with comparable-property evidence is meaningful. Four steps.

STEP 01 · 5 MIN

Find your county's last reassessment date.

Search "[your county] assessor's office last reassessment" or look at the bottom of your most recent property tax bill. The date will tell you how stale the system you're operating in is.

YOUR COUNTY ASSESSOR →
STEP 02 · 15 MIN

Pull three to five recent sale comps.

Free tools: county GIS portal, Zillow, Redfin. Find three to five recent sales of comparable properties in your immediate area. Note their sale price and assessed value. Calculate the ratio for each.

ZILLOW · COMP SEARCH →
STEP 03 · 30 MIN

File the appeal.

Most counties accept a one-page appeal form. PA counties typically have a March-to-August appeal window; NC counties are similar. You do not need a lawyer for residential appeals. The form takes 20-40 minutes to fill out with the comps.

ALLEGHENY APPEAL FORM →
STEP 04 · ONGOING

Push for a statutory cycle.

The deeper fix is at the state legislature, not the county. In PA, the bill that would mandate periodic reassessment has been introduced in successive sessions and consistently dies in committee. Find your state rep, ask where they stand.
