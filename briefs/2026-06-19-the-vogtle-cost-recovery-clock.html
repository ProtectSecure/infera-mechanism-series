In April 2009 the Georgia Public Service Commission authorized Georgia Power to charge ratepayers for two new Vogtle nuclear reactors before the plant produced any electricity. That mechanism, known as Construction Work In Progress, was supposed to last about seven years. It is now in its seventeenth. Each PSC docket that updates the Vogtle cost recovery has a brief intervention window. The window closes without notice. The rate adjustment lands on the bill.

SCENE · APRIL 2009 · ATLANTA

Five Public Service Commissioners gather in a hearing room downtown. The room is open to the public. Almost nobody is in the seats. On the agenda is a vote to authorize Georgia Power to begin charging its customers for the construction of two new nuclear reactors at Plant Vogtle in Waynesboro, three hundred miles south. The reactors do not yet exist. The cost estimate is roughly fourteen billion dollars.

The commissioners vote yes. Georgia becomes the first state in modern American history to authorize Construction Work In Progress cost recovery for nuclear power. The clock starts.

Seventeen years later, the reactors are finally running. The final cost is closer to thirty-five billion. The clock has not stopped.

EXHIBIT ATHE COST RECOVERY CLOCK

The CWIP clock has been running for seventeen years. The PSC has never paused it.

Construction Work In Progress flips the normal rule. Under standard utility regulation, a power company can only charge ratepayers for assets that are used and useful — operating, producing electricity. CWIP allows the utility to recover construction costs before the plant exists. In 2009, the Georgia legislature passed SB 31 authorizing this specifically for Vogtle. Every Georgia Power bill since has included a Vogtle line item.

Vogtle Unit 3 began commercial operation in 2023. Unit 4 followed in 2024. Until those dates, every dollar collected through CWIP was for an asset not yet producing electricity. The dates and the dollars are public; the PSC dockets that approved each successive rate adjustment are public; the intervention windows are public. Almost no Georgia Power customer has ever seen one.

EXHIBIT BDID YOU KNOW · A POP QUIZ

Three questions about who lets utilities do this, and who doesn't. Most people guess wrong on at least two.

Construction Work In Progress is not a Georgia invention; it is a tool that exists in every state but is regulated very differently. Take a guess before the reveal.

QUESTION 01 · HOW WIDESPREAD
How many U.S. states allow CWIP recovery for nuclear power plants under construction?
Six states. Georgia, South Carolina, Florida, Virginia, Mississippi, and (in narrow form) Kansas allow CWIP recovery for nuclear construction. Most states explicitly prohibit it. The reasoning behind the prohibition is older than nuclear: if a utility can charge for a plant that does not yet exist, the utility's incentive to control construction cost vanishes — there is no longer a meaningful gap between "build it cheaply" and "build it expensively." Source: Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis · multi-year survey
QUESTION 02 · COST OVERRUN
By how much did Vogtle Units 3 and 4 ultimately exceed the original 2009 cost estimate?
More than 150%. The original 2009 estimate was approximately $14 billion. The final cost is approximately $35 billion. That is the largest cost overrun in the modern history of American nuclear construction, and it is one of the largest cost overruns of any infrastructure project in U.S. history. The overrun was passed almost entirely to Georgia Power's customers through the CWIP mechanism — not absorbed by Southern Company shareholders. Source: Georgia Power PSC filings · S&P Global · Reuters
QUESTION 03 · WHO INTERVENED
In Vogtle's regular PSC cost recovery dockets since 2009, who has been the most active formal intervenor on behalf of residential ratepayers?
The Public Interest Advocacy Staff. Georgia is one of the few states where the PSC has an internal advocacy office tasked with representing the public in commission proceedings. PIA Staff has been the most consistent formal intervenor on Vogtle dockets since 2009 and has filed dissents on several major rate increases. Their dissents are public record. They have rarely been followed by the commissioners. Source: Georgia PSC docket review · 2009–2025

Atlanta is paying for a plant three hundred miles away.

Georgia Power is the dominant electric utility for nearly all of metro Atlanta. Approximately one million residential customers across Fulton County alone receive their power from Georgia Power. Every one of those customers pays the Vogtle cost recovery line.

The plant itself is in Waynesboro, in Burke County, on the South Carolina border. Most Atlanta-area Georgia Power customers will never see it. The economic relationship runs the other direction: the bill paid in Buckhead funded the concrete poured in Burke. The bill paid in Penn Hills, Pennsylvania funds the equivalent mechanism on the PJM grid — see Brief 003 on the PA PUC large-load tariff proceeding. Same mechanism. Different reactor.

If you live in Atlanta and you have a Georgia Power bill, you are a Vogtle investor whose participation was not optional. The amount each month is small; the cumulative amount is not. The intervention windows that decide whether the rate adjusts up or down are open right now in PSC dockets that no one is watching.

The pattern, five briefs in. Volume I is now sufficiently dense to draw the through-line clearly. Each clock is a statutory window that decides who pays for what, that the public has no way to see. Four of the five involve a ratepayer or taxpayer absorbing a cost they did not authorize for an asset that benefits a smaller, organized group. ACTIONBEFORE THE NEXT PSC DOCKET

The next Vogtle cost reconciliation will file. You can intervene. Most people don't.

Georgia PSC dockets accept written comments from any Georgia citizen. The Public Interest Advocacy staff cites volume of public comment. Four steps.

STEP 01 · 5 MIN

Find your most recent bill.

Locate the Nuclear Construction Cost Recovery (NCCR) or equivalent Vogtle line on your Georgia Power bill. It is typically near the bottom of the rate detail. The number per month is small; the cumulative number is not.

GEORGIA POWER →
STEP 02 · 10 MIN

Pull up the PSC docket search.

The Georgia PSC publishes every docket online with intervenor lists, comment deadlines, and full filings. Search for "Vogtle" or "Nuclear Construction Cost Recovery" to see the active proceeding.

GA PSC DOCKETS →
STEP 03 · 15 MIN

Read the PIA Staff filing.

The Public Interest Advocacy Staff files testimony and dissents on every Vogtle proceeding. Their filings name specific over-charges, methodology disputes, and reconciliation requests. They are written for the commission but understandable.

PIA STAFF FILINGS →
STEP 04 · 30 MIN

File a public comment.

Georgia residents can file public comments on PSC dockets through the PSC website or by email. You do not need to be an attorney. The comment goes on the public record and is cited by PIA Staff in their argument volume.

FILE A COMMENT →
