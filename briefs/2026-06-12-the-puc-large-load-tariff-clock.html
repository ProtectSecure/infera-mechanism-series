Five commissioners voted in April 2025 to ask one question.
Now your power bill is the answer.

In April 2025 the Pennsylvania PUC held an en banc hearing on who should pay for Big Tech's power. Amazon, Google, and Vantage Data Centers sent witnesses. Four electric utilities sent witnesses. Two state advocates spoke for sixty million household members. The comment window closed on June 23, 2025. Now the actual large-load tariffs are filing one by one, each with its own intervention window, and almost no one outside the docket knows.

SCENE · APRIL 24, 2025 · 1:00 PM

It is a Thursday afternoon at the Commonwealth Keystone Building in Harrisburg. Five commissioners sit at a curved desk in Hearing Room 1. There is a livestream nobody is watching. Outside the city, in Allegheny County, a substation hums for the city of Pittsburgh, the same hum it has made for 60 years. Across the Susquehanna, transformers are being measured for an expansion that does not yet exist on any map.

A motion has been filed. The motion asks: when a data center the size of a small town wants to plug into the grid, who pays for the grid?

By 5:00 PM, ten people will have answered. Two of them will be speaking for you.

EXHIBIT ATHE TARIFF CLOCK

So if the hearing already happened, where is the clock?

Most regulatory clocks have a visible deadline. A bill is introduced, hearings are scheduled, you can mark a date on a calendar. The PUC large-load tariff clock is different. It runs in the gap between when commissioners decide a thing in principle and when each electric utility translates that decision into an actual tariff filing. During that gap, the public has roughly 60 days per filing to formally intervene. By the time the higher bill shows up, the window has closed and the docket is decided.

THE WINDOW YOU CAN'T SEE

The en banc identified 15 tariff design questions. Each becomes a separate proceeding at a separate electric utility. Each has its own intervention window. There is no master calendar. Most ratepayers will not know the proceedings exist until the rate change appears on a statement.

EXHIBIT BDID YOU KNOW · A POP QUIZ

You are not the first state to do this. Most states already did it badly.

Pennsylvania's en banc is one of about a dozen state PUC proceedings on data-center tariffs that have happened since 2024. Each state's outcome depends almost entirely on who got a seat at the table. Three questions. See if the pattern is what you'd guess.

QUESTION 01 · NATIONAL
Since 2024, how many U.S. state PUCs have opened a formal proceeding on whether large-load (data center) customers should pay higher tariffs than residential ratepayers?
About twelve. Virginia, Ohio, Indiana, Georgia, Texas, Oregon, Washington, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee, New Jersey, and now Pennsylvania have all opened tariff proceedings on large-load customers between 2024 and 2026. The trigger in every case was the same: a wave of data-center interconnection requests that exceeded the entire current residential load of the host utility. Source: NARUC large-load tariff tracker · 2026
QUESTION 02 · WHO TESTIFIED
Of those proceedings, how many included formal testimony from Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta, or a hyperscaler trade group?
All twelve. Hyperscalers and their trade group (the Data Center Coalition) testified in every single state proceeding. They are organized, well-funded, and routinely send the same set of energy-policy specialists from one state hearing to the next. They have a clear interest and they show up to defend it. Source: Cross-state docket review · Infera 2026
QUESTION 03 · WHO SPOKE FOR HOUSEHOLDS
In how many of those 12 proceedings did residential ratepayers have a witness count equal to the hyperscalers'?
Zero. In every state, residential ratepayer interests were carried by a single Office of Consumer Advocate (or equivalent) representing the entire residential class. The hyperscalers typically had three to five witnesses. The asymmetry is not unique to Pennsylvania, and it is not a coincidence. Organized capital shows up to regulatory proceedings. Households do not. Source: Cross-state docket review · Harvard ELI

Duquesne Light Co. sent C. James Davis to the en banc.

He is Director of Rates, Energy Procurement, and Federal/RTO Affairs. He spoke about interconnection economics for large customers from a utility operations standpoint. He did a good job at his job. His job is to argue for Duquesne's preferred rate design.

His job is not to argue for the household in Lawrenceville running a window AC and a fridge. That job belongs to the Office of Consumer Advocate, who also showed up, with Acting Consumer Advocate Darryl Lawrence. The OCA represents every residential customer of every PA utility, which is, at last count, several million people.

The math here is not subtle. If you live in Allegheny County, you are paying Duquesne Light. Duquesne Light has a witness specifically arguing for what is good for Duquesne Light. You have one shared witness arguing for what is good for every residential customer in Pennsylvania. When the actual tariff files, the intervention window is ~60 days. It does not extend itself if you didn't see the email.

ACTIONWHAT TO DO BEFORE THE NEXT TARIFF FILES

The en banc is a closed proceeding. The downstream tariff filings are not. You can intervene. Most people don't.

The Office of Consumer Advocate is your second line of defense; you are your first. Four concrete steps, in order of effort.

STEP 01 · 2 MIN

Read the en banc record once.

The PUC's published page on the hearing has the agenda, every witness's testimony, and the Harvard paper. It is the entire case file in one place. You don't have to read it carefully; just see what's there.

EN BANC PAGE →
STEP 02 · 5 MIN

Subscribe to the docket.

The PUC's document search lets you watch a specific docket for new filings. Save Docket M-2025-3054271. When anything new posts, you'll be notified. This is how lobbyists know things; it costs nothing.

DOCKET M-2025-3054271 →
STEP 03 · 15 MIN

Find your utility's filings.

Search the PUC document search for your electric distribution company by name (Duquesne Light, PECO, FirstEnergy, PPL). When they file a large-load tariff, the case docket will say so in the title. Note the intervention deadline.

DOCUMENT SEARCH →
STEP 04 · 30 MIN

File a comment when it's time.

When a tariff files, the PUC accepts public comments through its eFiling system. You don't need a lawyer. A short, specific comment from a residential ratepayer is on the record forever, and the OCA cites volume.
