The One-Way Mirror

Ordinary people now sit in databases they cannot see: store watchlists, license plate logs, data broker files, a speed limiter's trip record. The officials who run or use those systems are building better ways to keep their own names out of view. This brief puts both sides of the glass in one place, along with the places where the public has started to win some sight back.

01The premise

Two sides of the same glass

A one-way mirror is a pane that is lit on one side and dark on the other. The lit side sees only itself; the dark side sees everything. Records about people now work much the same way. A store can flag your face and never tell you. A camera network can log your plate and share it across agencies. A broker can sell a profile you have never read. INFERENCE

At the same time, the officials who use those tools are arguing, often for real safety reasons, that their own names, faces and contact details should stay out of public view. Both claims can be partly right. The question this brief asks is narrower: who gets which protections, under which law, and who decides. INFERENCE

03The October leak

When the mirror cracked

In late October, the arrangement flipped for one agency. A group calling itself "Department of Peace" claimed it took a directory of Homeland Security staff, and the transparency collective DDoSecrets published it. Reporters counted 41,191 people, about 21,300 at Customs and Border Protection and roughly 10,000 at ICE including Homeland Security Investigations. The fields reported are work details: names, titles, components, managers, workplace addresses, work email and phone numbers, and internal group memberships. FACT Reporting describes workplace addresses, not home addresses. REPORTED Reuters matched six people against other data and confirmed details with three of them; the full archive has not been independently verified. REPORTED

  1. Oct. 28

    Publication. DDoSecrets posts the directory and a searchable site goes up. The hackers say the data came through an Okta sign-on service DHS uses; Okta told Reuters it has "no evidence" its systems were compromised. REPORTED

  2. Oct. 28 to 29

    DHS responds. A spokesperson says the department is "aware of a potential breach." Through Oct. 29 we found no confirmation of a breach and no named system. FACT

  3. Oct. 29

    Platforms act. Bluesky blocks links to the dataset and labels them "malicious," then says it will change the wording. Its chief operating officer says the files include contact details shared without consent. FACT

  4. Oct. 29

    The takedown. A security contractor, ZeroFox, files a notice on DHS's behalf. The .cx registry operator, CentralNic, places the search site's domain on hold. The Icelandic host, FlokiNET, refuses to remove the data, and the site returns at a new address. FACT

  5. The contract

    ICE and Homeland Security Investigations hold a ZeroFox contract with $13.0 million obligated, running July 2026 to July 2027. The Intercept tied it to a "Doxing Mitigation Initiative." A reported claim that ZeroFox may act for DHS until 2028 is not supported by the public contract records we found. FACT WATCH

Notice the tools. When officials' details leaked, the response ran through a private contractor, a domain registry's abuse policy and a social platform's rules. None of those routes is open in the same way to a shopper who wants off a store watchlist or a driver who wants a plate read deleted. 04What the law says

Public servants, partly public

Federal rules already split an employee's identity in two. Names, titles, grades, salaries and duty stations are public under an Office of Personnel Management rule. Direct phone numbers and email addresses are another matter: DHS's own FOIA policy says they should "generally be withheld." FACT That cuts against DDoSecrets' argument that public records requests require the leaked contact details. INFERENCE

05The case for the shield

Real threats, disputed numbers

DHS defends anonymity by pointing to threats against employees, which officials say have multiplied since immigration enforcement intensified in 2025, including incidents where officers' personal information was posted online and harassment at their homes. 07The trade-off

Washington bought privacy with sight

Washington state shows the two goals can collide. Courts ruled that Flock camera images were public records anyone could request. Cities paused cameras. Then the legislature passed SB 6002, which limits how plate data is kept and used, and also takes it out of public records law. Toggle to compare.

08What you can do

Clear a little of the glass

Californians

Sign up once at the state's DROP platform (privacy.ca.gov) to send a deletion request to every registered broker. Brokers must process it within their 45-day cycle. REPORTED

Vermonters

A right to delete broker data begins Jan. 1, 2027. Brokers must register and post a $20,000 bond. REPORTED

Marylanders

A 2026 bill to create a broker registry (SB 616 and HB 1220) got a hearing on Feb. 26; we found no record it passed. Ask your legislators whether it returns in 2027. WATCH

Everyone: plate readers

File a public records request with your police department for its plate reader policy, retention period and sharing list, and check your city council agenda for camera contracts. Washington now exempts the images themselves. INFERENCE

Everyone: stores

Look for biometric notices at entrances; New York City requires them. If you live in Illinois or Texas, state biometric laws apply. REPORTED Brief № 008 covers what to do if you are wrongly flagged.

Drivers under device orders

Ask the program in writing who receives your device log and how long it is kept. Maryland deletes within 30 days; Virginia's statute is silent. FACT

09Both sides

Shield the officer, or shield the public?

For protecting officials' identities

  • Officers report harassment at their homes and personal details posted online; families did not sign up for the job. REPORTED
  • The leaked directory reportedly includes undercover personnel, whose exposure can endanger operations and lives. REPORTED
  • Direct contact details were already withheld under DHS FOIA policy, so publishing them goes beyond what records law provides. FACT
  • The data was taken without authorization; platforms generally treat hacked personal data differently from reporting about it. INFERENCE

For transparency and the public's side

  • Names, titles and duty stations of federal employees are public by rule; accountability depends on knowing who acted. FACT
  • A federal court found the government had not shown that hiding identity is essential to enforcement. REPORTED
  • DHS's assault figures diverge sharply from court filings, which weakens claims built on them. REPORTED
  • The public gets no contractor, registry hold or platform block when its own data spreads; the asymmetry is the problem. INFERENCE
