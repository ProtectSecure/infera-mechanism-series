The Dilution

Four episodes followed one dollar through one room. This one pulls the camera back. Black intellectual property became the engine of American popular culture and a global export, and the opportunity that opened for everyone was real. What happened to the equity in ownership, each time the format changed, from the disc to the download to the stream to the model.

Hero: a generative circuit drawn in code, widening with each pass. No photograph, no real place.

AS OF Oct 30, 2026 READ ~50 min whole · 7 min per exhibit LAYER Public record · Census · SEC filings · RIAA · court opinions · trade data METHOD SVNIF

Three numbers from the same year. In 2024, R&B and hip-hop were 25.3 percent of everything streamed in the United States. Black-owned firms were 4.2 percent of the companies that make sound recordings. Black employer firms of every kind were about one percent of American business revenue. The culture was a quarter of the market; the deed to it was a rounding error. This episode is about how those three numbers came to sit so far apart, and what each change of format did to the gap.9, 7, 5

The argument is not that opportunity failed to grow. It grew enormously: for the player with a shoe, the student with a name to license, the producer with a laptop, the photographer with a camera, the kid with a dance. The argument is that each format change that widened the door also moved the till. The disc paid the shop; the stream pays the platform; the model pays whoever licensed the training set. Getty Images, which spent twenty years turning a photograph from a $536 license into a $21 one and then filed for bankruptcy the week this page was dated, is the worked example, because it is the industry that got to the end of the curve first.

The Driver

Instrument: the share of the market

Every number in this chapter is a share of something. The Selig Center's estimate of Black buying power went from $961 billion in 2010 to $1.6 trillion in 2020 to a Nielsen-cited $2.1 trillion, "up 2.4 times since 2000." Black employer firms went from 124,004 to 194,585 between 2017 and 2022, a 57 percent rise, and finished at about three percent of American firms and one percent of American business revenue. The demand side doubled. The ownership side stayed a rounding error.1, 2, 3, 5

FactThe Census counted 161,031 Black-owned employer firms in 2021 with $183.3 billion in receipts and 1.4 million employees; 194,585 in 2022 with $211.8 billion and 1.6 million. Seventy-one percent had one to nine employees; three percent had fifty or more. Health care was 26 percent of them; the Census does not break out arts, entertainment and recreation by the owner's race in the tables the public can reach.4, 5

FactThe National Endowment for the Arts, working from the 2017 survey, found Black owners held 4.2 percent of sound-recording businesses, 137 firms, about twice their two percent share of all businesses; minority owners held nine percent of the arts, entertainment and recreation sector.7 InferenceTwice the economy-wide share, in the industry Black music built, is 137 companies.

FactLuminate's 2024 count: 4.8 trillion on-demand audio streams worldwide, 1.4 trillion in the United States, and R&B and hip-hop first among genres at 341 billion, 25.3 percent of American streams. The share has fallen every year since 2020: down 1.7 points in 2023, 2.3 in 2024, another point in the first quarter of 2025. The single most-streamed track of 2024 was "Not Like Us," at 1.038 billion.8, 9, 10

ReportedThe export: Spotify says Afrobeats streams grew 5,022 percent between 2021 and 2025, Nigerian and South African royalties reached about $59 million in 2024, and Afrobeats streams in Latin America rose 400 percent since 2020. Black Panther grossed $700.4 million domestic and $1.35 billion worldwide in 2018. Run-DMC's Adidas deal in 1986 was $1 million; the five wealthiest hip-hop artists were worth about $4 billion combined by 2022.12, 13, 11

InferenceWhat the record does not contain is a dollar value for "the hip-hop economy." The figures that circulate are the whole American music industry's. The page uses the share, not the slogan.11 WatchThe Selig series before 2010, and the 2000 base behind "2.4 times," are not consistent across editions, and McKinsey's own estimate ran lower, $910 billion in 2019 toward $1.7 trillion by 2030; the chart below starts where the figures agree.1, 3, 6

The Ladder

Instrument: the royalty on a name

Sports is the industry where the opportunity grew fastest and the record is cleanest, because the leagues publish their splits. In 1984 a rookie signed with a shoe company for $250,000 a year plus a share of sales. In fiscal 2024 the brand with his name on it sold $8.7 billion; in fiscal 2025, $7.27 billion. He earned more than $200 million from the company in 2022 and about $300 million in 2024, mostly royalties, and sold the team he bought for $275 million at a $3 billion valuation.14, 15, 16

FactWhen that sale closed in June 2023, Andscape wrote that there was "no longer any majority African American ownership" in a league whose players were 71.8 percent Black. Twelve Black minority stakeholders remained across the league. Magic Johnson was "a significant financial participant" in the $6.05 billion Commanders purchase, percentage undisclosed. LeBron James's 2015 Nike contract is a lifetime deal whose value his partner put above a billion dollars without stating it.17, 18, 19, 20

FactThe players' share of league revenue, by collective bargaining: 49 to 51 percent in the NBA, 48 in the NFL, 50 in the NHL. The NBA's eleven-year media deals with Disney, NBC and Amazon were reported at about $76 billion. The NFL players' union took $297 million in group-licensing revenue in the year to February 2026, up 47 percent; one rookie's share was a record $17.7 million.21, 27, 25 InferenceHalf the revenue is the richest labor share in American entertainment. It is a wage, renegotiated on a schedule; the franchise appreciates to whoever holds it.

FactThe students: name, image and likeness money went from $918 million in 2021 to 22 to $2.26 billion in 2024 to 25, with collectives paying 81.6 percent of it and football taking 72 percent of collective dollars. The House settlement, approved June 6, 2025: $2.8 billion in back pay over ten years and a $20.5 million per-school revenue-sharing cap, with 83.9 percent of the shared dollars expected to go to football and men's basketball. EA paid about 11,000 players $600 and a copy of the game for College Football 25, then $1,500 for the sequel. Flat fees, no royalty.22, 23, 24

ReportedThe secondary market: StockX was valued at $3.8 billion in 2021; global sneaker resale went from about $6 billion in 2019 to $10.6 billion in 2022, with Air Jordan and Nike at 71 percent of it, and the Air Jordan 1 alone at about 23 percent at a 54 percent average premium.26 InferenceThe most valuable name in sports IP earns a royalty on the first sale and nothing on the resale, which is a $10 billion market priced on the name.

WatchThe royalty rate on the shoe has never been published; "about five percent" circulates without a source, and the 2024 earnings against fiscal-2025 revenue work out near 3.4 percent, which is our arithmetic and not the contract. Jordan Brand's fiscal-2024 figure exists on two reporting bases, $7.0 and $8.7 billion; the page uses Nike's restated one and does not mix them.15, 16

The Shelf

Instrument: the format

A shelf is a place where a copy sits and someone owns it. Recorded music in the United States peaked at about $22.4 billion in inflation-adjusted terms in 1999, fell to $7.2 billion in 2014, about a third below the peak, and came back to $17.7 billion in 2024 with streaming at 84 percent of it and 100 million paid subscriptions. The money returned. It returned to a different till.28, 29

FactThe 2025 year-end, on the wholesale basis the RIAA now publishes: $11.535 billion, streaming 82.1 percent, 106.5 million paid subscriptions providing 55.3 percent of revenue; physical $1.381 billion, of which vinyl $1.043 billion on 46.8 million units and the CD $312 million, down 7.8 percent; downloads $272.6 million. The first half of 2026 was $6 billion, up 6.9 percent.30

FactCiti's 2018 study put the whole American music economy at $43 billion in 2017 and the artists' capture at 12 percent, about $5 billion; labels and publishers took about $10 billion. Spotify said it paid out more than $10 billion in 2024, about $60 billion cumulative; roughly 1,500 artists earned a million dollars or more on it, 12,500 earned $100,000, and independents took about half the payouts.31, 32 InferenceTwelve cents on the dollar is the structure of Episode 3 at the scale of an economy. The platform publishes who it paid; the record does not say what share of the 1,500 are Black.

FactThe disc, in film: the DEG's count of American disc sales peaked above $16 billion in 2005 and fell below $1 billion in 2024, down 23.4 percent in one year and 93.75 percent from the peak; Variety wrote that the DVD business died in 2024. In December 2023 Sony told PlayStation owners that 1,318 purchased seasons of Discovery programming would be removed on New Year's Eve under "content licensing arrangements," with no refund, and reversed only after an eleventh-hour deal with the rights holder.33, 34

ReportedThe disc, in games: physical spending in the United States fell by more than half from 2021 and more than 85 percent from its 2008 peak; Capcom's unit sales are about 90 percent digital, Sony's software about 82 percent, and Sony plans to end disc releases from January 2028.35 InferenceThe copy the customer owned is becoming the license the customer rents, in every medium at once. The shelf was where a Black-owned shop, label or distributor sat between the work and the buyer; the stream has no shelf.

The Screen

Instrument: the median

Black Americans spend 81 hours a week with media, a third more than the general population; 73 percent pay for three or more streaming services; streaming is 45.9 percent of Black adults' television time, more than cable and broadcast combined. The audience is the heaviest in the country. UCLA's annual report has measured, for a decade, whether the industry has built for it.40

FactThe 2025 report, on 2024's films: casts that were 41 to 50 percent people of color posted a record median global box office of $234.6 million; casts 11 to 20 percent posted the lowest, $33.3 million; people of color were the majority of opening-weekend ticket buyers for 12 of the top 20. The same report: films with fewer than 11 percent actors of color rose from 8.5 to 18.4 percent of releases; the white share of theatrical roles went from 59.6 to 67.2 percent; writers of color fell from 22.2 to 12.5 percent; majority-POC casts were the worst performers for the first time since 2015, half of them opened in fewer than 3,000 theaters.38

ReportedThe 2026 report, on 2025's 109 films: people of color about 23 percent of leads, directors of color about 22 percent, and the 41-to-50-percent band again the highest median at home and abroad; Sinners the outlier.39 InferenceThe market rewards the mix and the industry retreats from it in the same twelve months. The dilution here is not of a deed but of a greenlight.

FactThe 2023 strikes ended with a writers' contract that pays a 50 percent bonus on the fixed streaming residual when a title is watched by 20 percent of domestic subscribers within 90 days, about $9,031 for a half hour and $16,415 for an hour, requires the studios to disclose hours streamed, and says a machine cannot be a writer and its output is not source material. The performers' contract added a $40 million-a-year streaming participation fund for three years, a seven percent minimum raise, and consent and payment for digital replicas, living or dead.36, 37

ReportedThe pipe under it: pay television at 43.2 million homes, 34 percent penetration, against about 105 million around 2010; 64.8 million counting the virtual bundles. The regional sports networks' parent went through a twenty-month bankruptcy that erased more than $9 billion of debt, nine baseball teams terminated their deals in January 2026, and the last broadcast aired on April 30, 2026.41, 42 InferenceThe cable dollar that funded BET, OWN and the overall deals of Episode 3 is leaving the system at about three million homes a year.

The Stock Image

Instrument: the license fee

The stock photograph is the one creative product that has already been through every format in this episode: the print, the disc, the download, the subscription and the model. In 1989 a rights-managed photograph in a national ad licensed for $1,375 to $4,125. In 2006 Getty's average rights-managed fee was $536.12 and the photographer's royalty about $268. In 2020, by a contributor's sample of his own statements, the average Getty license was $21.06 and the royalty, at 20 percent, $4.21. Half of licenses were under $10; fewer than one in a hundred over $1,000.43

FactThe disc did it first: royalty-free CD-ROMs sold for about $300, the creator took 20 percent of disc revenue, about 20 cents an image on a 300-image disc. Then the download: microstock paid under 50 cents. Getty bought iStockphoto for $50 million in February 2006, the year its creative revenue was $634.1 million on a collection of 1.76 million images; the collection grew to 29.4 million and creative revenue fell under $280 million. Long-term contributors, by the same sample, earn a tenth of their 2006 income.43, 44

FactThe owners: Hellman & Friedman took the company private for about $2.4 billion in 2008; Carlyle bought it for $3.3 billion in 2012, $2.6 billion of that debt; the Getty family retook the majority in 2018; a SPAC merger valued it at $4.8 billion in July 2022, and the stock went from $10 to $32 in a week and $8.49 by September. Three sales, $10.5 billion in enterprise value, in fourteen years, while the fee on the product went from $536 to $21.45

FactThe subscription: in 2024 Shutterstock replaced its flat 30 percent contributor royalty with tiers of 20 to 40 percent of an assigned value of 45 to 81 cents a download; a worked example went from 31 cents to 13. Getty's own restructuring deck says contracted contributors "typically receive 20 to 50 percent of the license fee," plus a payment when their content is used in a training set.46, 47

FactThe model: Getty sued Stability AI in Delaware in February 2023 over about 12 million scraped images, dismissed and refiled in San Francisco in August 2025, trial set for January 18, 2028. In London, on November 4, 2025, the High Court found a trained model is not an "infringing copy" because it never stores the pictures, and gave Getty only "extremely limited" trademark findings on watermarks in two old versions. Getty launched its own generator in September 2023, trained on part of its 477 million assets, paying contributors a recurring share by formula. Shutterstock earned $104 million licensing its library to AI developers in 2023 and projected $250 million a year by 2027; contributor payouts undisclosed.48, 49, 51, 52

FactThe end of the curve: a $3.7 billion merger with Shutterstock announced January 2025, cleared by the Justice Department unconditionally and by Britain's regulator on condition of a divestiture, and terminated on July 7, 2026 when Getty declined it. A going-concern warning in March 2026 citing debt, merger costs, litigation and AI competition. On October 21, 2026 the company filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 in Delaware: $1.46 billion of funded debt, the Getty family and Koch to take 100 percent of the equity with up to $533 million of new money, unsecured notes recovering about 63 cents, the public shares and warrants cancelled. Revenue in 2025 was $981.3 million; the deck names generative AI "a structural industry headwind." The company said its relationships with photographers were expected to be unaffected. No contributor terms were published.53, 54, 50, 47

InferenceEvery format change widened the door: anyone with a camera could sell, and 29 million images did. Every format change cut the fee by an order of magnitude and moved the margin to the aggregator, which was sold three times to funds and then handed, by a court, to its founders and a conglomerate's investment arm. The photographer's royalty is the only line in the story that never had a buyer.

The Machine

Instrument: the training set

In January 2025, ten percent of the tracks uploaded to Deezer each day were made by a model, about 10,000. In April it was 18 percent; in September, 28; in January 2026, 39; in April 2026, 44 percent, 75,000 a day; in June 2026, more than half, about 90,000 a day. The platform now removes machine-made tracks tied to fraudulent streams and any that go six months unplayed. Spotify said it had removed more than 75 million "spammy" tracks in a year and banned unauthorized voice clones. An AI band passed 500,000 monthly listeners within weeks in July 2025.56, 57, 58

FactThe first fake was April 4, 2023: a track in the voices of Drake and the Weeknd reached about 600,000 Spotify streams and 15 million TikTok views before Universal had it taken down on the 17th, saying training on its artists "breaches our agreements and violates copyright law." In June 2024 the three majors sued Suno and Udio for up to $150,000 a work. Universal settled with Udio on October 29, 2025; Warner with Udio on November 19 and Suno on November 25, as licensing partnerships, the Suno figure reported at $500 million. Sony did not settle: it filed a second suit against Udio in July 2026 and, with Universal, a second against Suno over 60,202 recordings in September.55

FactThe statutes: Tennessee's ELVIS Act, signed March 21, 2024, the first state law adding a voice to likeness protection against cloning. The NO FAKES Act of 2026 advanced unanimously from Senate Judiciary on June 18; the House has not taken it up; it is a bill. SAG-AFTRA members ratified the video-game contract on July 9, 2025 by 95 percent, ending a 320-day strike: written consent and disclosure for replicas, 7.5 times scale for real-time generation, the right to suspend consent in a strike. In September the union said of a synthetic "actress" pitched to agencies that it was "not an actor."59, 60, 61

FactThe courts: a judge approved Anthropic's $1.5 billion settlement with authors in September 2025, about $3,000 a book across 465,000 works, after holding that training was fair use but storing seven million pirated books was not. The Times' case against OpenAI survived its motion to dismiss in March 2025 and reached cross-motions on fair use in September 2026. Disney, Universal and Warner Bros. Discovery's consolidated case against Midjourney closes expert discovery on November 9, 2026 with no trial date. Getty's is set for 2028.62, 63, 64, 48

ReportedThe estimates, each from an interested party: CISAC's commissioned study puts creators' cumulative loss at €22 billion by 2028, 24 percent of music creators' revenue and 21 percent of audiovisual at risk, with generative music providers going from €0.1 billion to €4 billion; the animation guilds' study puts 204,000 American entertainment jobs disrupted by 2026; Goldman Sachs put 300 million jobs worldwide exposed.65 InferenceThe pattern of the five exhibits before this one: the label settles for the catalog; the aggregator licenses the library; the platform sets the filter; the statute makes the voice a tradeable right. The creator who was never at the table in Episode 3 is not at this one either. The opt-in is the door; the deed to the training set is the thing the door opens onto.

Exhibit 7 of 71984 to 2026Four industries · one instrumentSynthesis

The Dilution

Instrument: opportunity against ownership

The thesis, stated as the record supports it. In each industry this episode measured, the popularization of Black intellectual property widened the door for everyone: more players paid, more students paid, more songs uploaded, more photographers selling, more films that sold on a mixed cast. In each, the format change that widened the door moved the margin from the holder of a copy to the operator of a system, and the system was owned elsewhere.5, 22, 29, 43

InferenceThree dilutions, in order. The first was the disc to the download: the shop and the independent distributor lost the shelf, and the independent label of Episode 3 lost the thing it had kept its masters for. The second was the download to the stream: the copy became a license, the platform took the till, and the artist's capture of the whole settled near twelve cents. The third is the stream to the model: the catalog became a training set, licensed by its holder, and the creator's name became an opt-in.

FactWhat did not dilute: the wage. Players at 48 to 51 percent of league revenue; a $297 million licensing pool; a $40 million streaming fund; $2.26 billion in NIL. The share of income that is bargained collectively rose in every industry where there was a union or a players' association to bargain it.21, 25, 37, 22 InferenceThe equity in opportunity grew where it was negotiated as a wage. The equity in ownership diluted where it was held as a copy. The two moved in opposite directions through the same formats.

WatchFour open matters decide the next format. Getty's 2028 trial and the Times' fair-use motions decide whether a training set needs a license. NO FAKES decides whether a voice is a tradeable deed, and the contracts of Episode 3 decide who trades it. Sony's second suits decide whether the label's settlement is the industry's. And the Census's next survey decides whether the 194,585 became more than one percent of the revenue.48, 63, 60, 55, 5

Closing

Three findings, one direction

What the record supports, and what it does not.

The door opened on every ladder and the top rung stayed where it was. Each format change moved the till from the holder of a copy to the operator of a system. The stock image got to the end of the curve first, and the end of the curve is a bankruptcy court handing a company back to its founders with the contributors unmentioned. What held was the wage, wherever there was someone to bargain it. What diluted was the deed, wherever it was held as a copy.

What the record supports

  • The Census counts, the RIAA year-ends, Nike's fiscal release, the House settlement, the UK judgment, the Getty petition and deck, and the union contracts are primary (T1) or contemporaneous press (T2).
  • Luminate's genre shares, Opendorse's NIL totals and the DEG's disc figures are the trade's own counts and are chipped T4.
  • Every valuation of Getty is a reported transaction; every license fee is from a contributor's published sample of his own statements, and is labeled as such.

What it does not

  • No source gives a dollar value for "the hip-hop economy"; the Selig series before 2010 is inconsistent across editions; the Jordan royalty rate has never been published.
  • The Census does not break out Black ownership of arts, entertainment and recreation firms in the public tables; the NEA's 4.2 percent is from 2017.
