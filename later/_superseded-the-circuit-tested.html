The Circuit Tested

Black art, Black money and the movement have run on one circuit for a century: the performer, the room, the audience, the outside dollar that came to watch. Seven exhibits on what happened when that circuit was put under load, from the Los Angeles verdict of 1992 to the last week of October 2026.

Hero: a generative circuit drawn in code, one segment cut. No photograph, no real place.

AS OF Oct 30, 2026 READ ~40 min whole · 5–6 min per exhibit LAYER Public record · Census · FEC · court files · press METHOD SVNIF

A circuit carries current only while every segment holds. The one this episode follows was built in segregated rooms: a performer, a room that would book them, an audience that paid, an outside dollar that came in to watch, and a church and a movement that drew on all of it. By 1968 it was running at national scale. This episode asks what each stress since 1992 did to it. Three answers, and the evidence for each.

The method is the same as Episode 1. Every figure carries a label. Political content is descriptive: what each candidate, party, artist and company said and did, and what the pollsters and the Census counted. Where the pollsters disagree they are named one by one. Where the record is silent the page says so rather than fill it, and where it is contested it carries both numbers.

READING MEMO · Every exhibit stands alone. Fact is on the record, Reported is a credible account we could not independently confirm, Inference is our reading, Watch is open as of the dateline. Three tags run through the page: TESTED means the circuit was put under load, EVOLVED means a new route opened, MUTED means a route closed. The tags are this page's reading of each event; the dates and numbers are from the record. The midterm election is November 3, 2026, four days after the dateline, and nothing here predicts it. This is an original Infera investigation from the public record; for the long history of Black creative ownership it runs alongside, see Lisa E. Davis, Our Minds Were Always Free (Simon & Schuster, 2026), listed in the sources as a further read.85

The Verdict

Instrument: the shareholder meeting

The art had already done the reporting. What the verdict of April 29, 1992 tested was not whether a song could say what the city knew. It was who could be made to pay for its saying so, and the answer came from three places the movement's treasury did not control: a boardroom, a nominee's podium and a late-night couch.

FactIn August 1989, seven months after Straight Outta Compton became a two-million seller, an FBI assistant director wrote Priority Records a three-paragraph letter: "Advocating violence and assault is wrong, and we in the law enforcement community take exception." He admitted he had not heard the song. An informal police fax network urged venues to cancel the tour; Detroit police rushed the Joe Louis Arena stage.3 InferenceThe fax network is the template every later boycott of a Black artist would use: organized pressure on the venue and the corporation, not on the artist. In November 1991 some 3,000 Korean-American stores boycotted St. Ides over Ice Cube's Black Korea; the brewer dropped his ads and pledged a community fund within weeks.4, 93

FactThe verdict's toll is printed as 53, 55 and 63 in reputable sources, a reclassification dispute; damage of $800 million to $1 billion; more than 2,300 Korean-owned businesses damaged, about 45% of the total.1, 2 Rebuild LA promised $6 billion of private investment; about $1 billion was pledged and $382 million arrived. Vons pledged ten to twelve supermarkets and built one. John Mack of the Urban League: "I said, Peter, this is not the Olympics."9, 10

FactSeven months after the fires, Ice Cube's The Predator, with a track addressing the acquitted officers, debuted at No. 1 with 193,000 copies. The Chronic, which samples the riot documentary Birth of a Nation 4×29×92, reached 5.7 million U.S. copies; Death Row, financed by a $10 million Interscope deal after Sony passed, peaked above $100 million a year.11 Spike Lee's Malcolm X opened that November after Warner capped the budget at about $30 million and Bill Cosby, Oprah Winfrey, Prince, Janet Jackson and Magic Johnson covered the overruns.12 InferenceThe decade's biggest commercial pivot was routed through a Time Warner-affiliated label, which is the lever Exhibit 2 is about.

The Re-plumbing

Instrument: the Telecommunications Act of 1996

Nothing silenced the art. What happened in the nineties was a change of pipe: the chokepoint moved from the label board to the distribution layer to the ownership ledger, and the political energy that had lived in lyrics moved into registration machinery. Each valve below is dated; the direction of each is in its color.

FactMay 31, 1995, Los Angeles: Bob Dole named Time Warner and Ice-T, the Geto Boys and 2 Live Crew: "Must you debase our nation and threaten our children for the sake of corporate profits?" He also conceded in the same speech that "movies and music do not make children into murderers."13 C. DeLores Tucker of the National Political Congress of Black Women and William Bennett opened their campaign the same month; in August Interscope and Death Row sued Tucker, alleging she had sought her own distribution deal.15 On September 27 Time Warner sold its half of Interscope, valued at $115 million. Dole: "Time Warner has felt the sting of shame."14

InferenceA bipartisan, cross-racial coalition moved $115 million of corporate capital with no statute and no verdict. Shame is cheaper than law. The evenhanded reading: the campaign had Black institutional backing, and the label's counter-suit alleged the campaign was itself a money play. Both are in the record. So is this: the 1994 Crime Bill, the policy hip-hop most consistently criticized, passed with a majority of the Congressional Black Caucus voting yes and 58% Black support in Gallup.16 The Million Man March of October 16, 1995 drew 400,000 by the Park Police count and 837,000 by Boston University's; the Park Police stopped issuing counts afterward.83

The ownership floor

What the 1996 Act did to who owned the room. Toggle the measure.

FactRadio One's 1999 Nasdaq listing at $24 reached $96.50 in seven months; it bought twelve Clear Channel stations for $1.3 billion in 2000 and peaked at 70 stations in 2007.21 The Act had lifted the 40-station cap; owners fell from about 5,100 to 3,800 by 2001, and Black-owned full-power television stations from 25 to 18 between 1998 and 2006.17, 19 BET, founded in 1980 with about $500,000, sold to Viacom in 2000 for about $3 billion. Cathy Hughes: "a great day for Bob Johnson, but … a significant loss for African American ownership."22, 23 InferenceOne Black consolidator rode the wave that shrank the rest; the largest Black media asset became one fortune and one Viacom line item; political programming was the first casualty of the sale. The 2007 floor is the base from which Exhibit 7's 2025 retrenchments should be read.

FactThe contrast case: in June 2000 Bruce Springsteen's American Skin (41 Shots), about Amadou Diallo, drew a boycott call from the PBA and the New York FOP and a request from Mayor Giuliani not to play it. All ten Madison Square Garden shows sold out; no label action followed.24 InferenceA white rock star faced the Ice-T playbook eight years later and lost no label and no sales. The record does not explain the difference; it only shows it.

Reconnected

Instrument: the endorsement

Between the 2004 registration drives and the 2009 inaugural stages, every element of the segregated-era circuit reappeared at national scale with the signs reversed. The performer became the donor and the ticket-seller. The outside money came in four million small pieces. The telethon was the one channel that could not be pre-cleared, and it was muted anyway, by three hours of tape.

Fact2004: Citizen Change ("Vote or Die") and the Hip-Hop Summit Action Network claimed, respectively, 250,000 and "nearly 2 million" registrations; Citizen Change disbanded after the election.25 Census and CIRCLE: turnout among 18-to-24-year-olds rose eleven points, from 36% to 47%, the largest gain of any age group.26 InferenceThe surge was real and the registration claims were self-reported with no audit, a pattern that recurs in every celebrity-driven drive on this page. September 20, 2007: the Jena Six rally drew 15,000 to 20,000 to a town of 3,000; Al Sharpton credited "black Internet blogs, e-mail, and talk radio."92

The Dimmer

Instrument: the Census turnout table

With a Black president, the circuit's treasury end and its stage end were briefly the same building, and that alignment produced the one measurable peak in the whole record. It is also where the movement stopped waiting for the stage. This exhibit puts three dials beside every election since 2008 and asks which one the turnout followed.

FactIn 2012 Black turnout, 66.2%, exceeded non-Hispanic white turnout, 64.1%, for the first time the Census had recorded.39 On September 18, 2012 Jay-Z and Beyoncé hosted a $40,000-a-ticket fundraiser at the 40/40 Club, reported at about $4 million.40 InferenceThe performer who in 1955 was the thing being ticketed is now the host writing the ticket. That is the clearest marker of the ownership shift Episode 1 traces, and it coincides with the peak.

The Pledge

Instrument: the contract

The circuit's outside-money end, which in the 1950s meant a white audience buying a ticket and a Northern donor writing a check, became corporate balance sheets writing pledges. The scale was unprecedented; the verification was not. The sports front showed every stage of the cycle in miniature: protest, administrative closure, market reward, private settlement, contract, and then the largest audience in history for the protest's own imagery.44, 45, 46

Fifteen Weeks

Instrument: the exit poll

Within four years the 2020 pledges were being reversed by statute, litigation and shareholder politics. Within fifteen weeks of July 21, 2024 the circuit was run at the highest voltage in its history: a Black candidate, more than a billion dollars, nearly every major Black performer on the stage. Black turnout came in at the 2016 level. This exhibit reports what each side said and did and what the pollsters counted. It does not pick a cause.61, 62, 63, 64, 65

FactJune 29, 2023: SFFA v. Harvard ended race-conscious admissions. August 2023 to September 2024: litigation shut the Fearless Fund's $20,000 grant for Black women founders. November 2024 to January 2025: Walmart, McDonald's, Meta and Target ended DEI goals; Costco and Apple shareholders rejected anti-DEI proposals.54, 55, 56 InferenceThe 2020 commitments reversed inside the same corporate cycle that made them. The retreat was not universal.

FactJuly 21: Biden withdrew; the "Win With Black Women" call drew about 44,000 and raised about $1.5 million that night. July 22: Beyoncé cleared Freedom; July 23: Harris walked out to it in Wilmington. July 30: Megan Thee Stallion in Atlanta. July 31: at the NABJ convention Trump said Harris "happened to turn Black." May 23 and June 15: Trump's Bronx rally with drill rappers Sheff G and Sleepy Hallow, and his Detroit church visit. October 25: Beyoncé in Houston, "I'm not here as a celebrity … I'm here as a mother"; she did not perform.57

The Board

Instrument: the executive order

For the first time since the 1950s the federal end of the circuit, the stages, museums and grants opened to Black performance across six decades, was being closed, renamed or reviewed, while the commercial stage kept its largest audiences in history and found a new, non-racial protest theme. This exhibit is a lighting board. Slide the year and watch which lights are on.

Three findings, one direction

What the record supports, and what it does not.

The circuit was never silenced; it was re-plumbed, and at every stage the art kept selling while the room changed hands. The stage can be lit to any brightness and the record shows it does not, by itself, move the audience to the polls; the one peak belonged to a candidate. The oldest tools, the church and the registration line, still move numbers; the newest, the pledge and the contract, are the least audited things on this page. And the federal stage that opened to Black performance between 1939 and 2016 is, as of the dateline, closed, renamed or under review, while the commercial stage has never had a larger audience. Where the current goes next is not in the record. The midterm is Tuesday.

What the record supports

  • The Census turnout series, the FEC filings, the Supreme Court opinion, the executive orders and the Garthwaite–Moore estimate are primary (T1).
  • The three pollsters agree on the direction of the 2024 Black male shift; they are named one by one and their levels and shifts are never mixed.
  • Every pledge is shown with its delivery figure or a hatched bar; none is counted as spent.

What it does not

  • Why Black turnout in 2024 equalled 2016's. The page declines to say what the record does not.
  • Whether the Target boycott caused Target's results; whether the Roc Nation deal was capture or conquest; whether the 2025 silence from the stage is strategy or fatigue.
  • The 1992 death toll, the Million Man March count, the Oprah invoice and the "$20 million on celebrities" line are disputed and carried as such. Sexyy Red (voted Harris) and Nicki Minaj (aligned in 2025, not 2024) were retired from an earlier brief.
