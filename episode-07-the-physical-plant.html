I.· THE ANATOMY · WHAT'S INSIDE THE BUILDING

A building you have never been allowed inside.

300,000 to 500,000 square feet. 30 to 100 megawatts of critical IT load. Server racks at 40 kW each — four times what your data center looked like five years ago. Almost no people. Sized to run on its own diesel for 96 hours.

II.· THE OWNERSHIP · THE FOUR COMPANIES

Four hyperscalers. Four REITs. One concentration.

The hyperscalers appear in The Data Center Bill as customers driving Georgia load. In The Physical Plant they appear as owners of the buildings — different editorial work, sharper political-economic claim.

The hyperscalers own the buildings.

Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, and Meta build and operate their own facilities for their own workloads and for cloud customers. The four-company share of US hyperscale capacity is consistently in the 60–75% range, with build-cycle fluctuations.

The REIT layer is consolidating.

Equinix, Digital Realty, CyrusOne (acquired by KKR + Global Infrastructure Partners in November 2021), and Iron Mountain. The colocation REIT market consolidated significantly in 2021–2024. The four-company-plus-four-REIT structure is unlikely to expand and likely to contract.

GovCloud sits inside the same buildings.

AWS GovCloud, Microsoft Azure Government, and Google Public Sector operate inside the same architectural pattern as their commercial counterparts. The FedRAMP authorization boundaries are administrative and logical, not necessarily physical.

III.· THE PATTERN · THE GEORGIA MECHANISM IS THE NATIONAL MECHANISM

Same statute. Same forecast. Same reform. Same death.

The opposition isn't local. The reform isn't local. The blocker isn't local. The architecture is.

Every major host state has a small bipartisan caucus introducing the same three-piece reform agenda — hyperscale tariff, tax-incentive sunset, per-facility disclosure. Bills die in committee in each state. The blocker pattern (PSC commissioner composition + donor architecture + low-turnout elections) is structurally similar across jurisdictions. IV.· THE CONVERGENCE · WHERE FRAGMENTATION COLLAPSES

The legal fragmentation does not exist at the physical layer.

EP 06 named legal-data fragmentation — federal separate from state separate from local separate from commercial — as a structural defense. It is a real defense at the legal frame. It is not a defense at the physical frame.

The buildings hosting your commercial composite are often the same buildings hosting your civic composite. The legal fragmentation says "no single authority can integrate your file." The physical reality says "the integration is one rack away from being possible."

What this piece marks rather than asserts.

What we did NOT prove

  • That any specific hyperscaler exceeded authority in any specific siting decision.
  • That the political-economy relationships are intentionally opaque rather than accidentally complex.
  • That the 2028 energy share projection is the most likely outcome — projections are projections.
  • That GovCloud + commercial co-location is being used to integrate fragmented files. We showed the physical capability exists.

What we DID prove

  • The buildings exist; ownership concentration is real and quantifiable.
  • The Georgia rate-payer subsidy mechanism (S1·07) reproduces in every other major host state with documented variants.
  • The same three-piece reform agenda is being introduced and dying in committee in every host state for structurally similar reasons.
  • Physical convergence of commercial and civic composites at the data-center level is verifiable through FedRAMP records.
  • The next reactor cycle is initiating, with AI compute as the demand anchor.

Hosted defenders · the counter-read

Hyperscaler industry voice

Data centers generate net economic benefit through property tax revenue, construction jobs, and skilled long-term employment. Tax incentives are competitive with peer states and reflect the value of the strategic asset being attracted. Grid investments serve all customers downstream.

Federalism / state-rights voice

State-level variation in data-center policy is a feature of US federalism, not a flaw. National-scale criticism overstates uniformity and underweights local democratic agency.

AI-competitiveness voice

US ability to host AI compute at scale is a national-economic and national-security priority. Restricting data-center growth through tariff isolation cedes that capacity to other jurisdictions.
