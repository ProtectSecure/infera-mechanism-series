A donor-committee graph · structural, not allegational

The committees that regulate them share donor lists with them. The graph is the question, not the verdict.

Which Georgia legislators sit on the committees that oversee the industries that fund their campaigns. The overlap is structural and computable. Whether it represents capture, alignment, or expertise-driven self-selection is a question the graph itself does not resolve — and the piece refuses to pre-resolve.

i.
The bodyAct 01

Two hundred thirty-six elected members.

The Georgia General Assembly: 56 senators and 180 House members. Each runs a campaign. Each accepts donations. Each is assigned to committees. The committees decide which bills live and die.

The work of legislation is done in committee. Floor votes ratify what committees produce. Committee chairs control the calendar, the witness list, the markup, the death of a bill.

SOURCE · GA General Assembly rosters
ii.
The committeesAct 02

Each committee oversees an industry surface.

Insurance committees regulate insurance. Banking committees regulate banking. Energy committees regulate utilities. Transportation regulates transportation. The mapping is not metaphorical; it is statutory. Industries get oversight where their bills get heard.

Committee assignment is the first lever of regulatory influence. The second is who chairs.

SOURCE · GA House/Senate committee rosters
iii.
The donationsAct 03

Donations arrive from regulated industries.

Campaign finance filings, public via the Georgia Government Transparency and Campaign Finance Commission, show donor employer information for many contributions. Insurance executives donate to insurance committee members. Utility employees and PACs donate to energy committee members. Some of this is unavoidable — industries have interests; political contributions are legal speech. The question is the shape and the density.

SOURCE · GA Government Transparency Commission
iv.
The graphAct 04

The graph builds itself.

Render every legislator as a node, every donor as a node, every committee as a containing region. Draw an edge from a donor to every legislator they fund. Color the edge by dollar value. The clusters that emerge are the policy areas where industry-elected-official alignment is densest.

Sometimes the alignment reflects voter preference (constituents in a district full of utility workers fund their utility-oversight legislator). Sometimes it reflects something else. The graph does not adjudicate. It shows the structure that adjudication would have to engage.

SOURCE · multi-source linkage
v.
The billsAct 05

Bills move through. Some die in specific places.

Layer in the legislative session: every bill introduced, which committee it landed in, which chair controlled its calendar, which bills passed, which died, which were amended into something different than they started. The pattern of which bills die in which committees, by which chairs, by which industry-funded actors, is computable from public records.

The signal is structural, not individual. No single donation explains any single vote. The body-wide pattern across a multi-session window is what statehouse reporters call a beat. Right now nobody in Georgia is publishing it as a graph.

SOURCE · GA legislative bill tracker
vi.
The franchiseAct 06

This is a recurring beat, not a one-shot piece.

Republish each session. The graph evolves; the format does not. As committee assignments shift, as legislators retire or rise, as new donors emerge and old ones fade, the structural pattern adjusts. Whoever owns this format owns one of the cleanest accountability beats available in modern state-level politics.

The 2027 session begins in January. Committee assignments for 2027 will be made in late 2026 — when readers will most want to know which assignments matter, and what they correlate with.

What else could explain the donor-committee overlap?

The capture-graph framing is one read of the committee-donor structure. Three alternate framings produce the same graph from different causal premises.

A · this piece

Donor money shapes committee work

Industries fund the legislators on the committees that oversee them, creating influence that flows from donor to bill outcome.

DISTINGUISHING TESTDo committee-member voting records on industry-relevant bills correlate with received donations after controlling for partisan baseline?
B · expertise-driven

Committee assignment follows expertise and constituency

Legislators with backgrounds or districts tied to an industry self-select onto its committee. Donations follow alignment rather than create it.

DISTINGUISHING TESTAmong legislators who joined an industry committee, do donations precede or follow the assignment by a meaningful interval?
C · alignment-not-influence

Donations chase alignment, not vice versa

Industries donate to legislators who already share their positions. The graph shows alignment; it does not show influence.

DISTINGUISHING TESTIf industry-aligned legislators were randomly reassigned to non-industry committees, would their voting records change?
D · power-concentration

Chairs are the real lever, members are noise

Committee membership matters less than chair selection. The graph as drawn overstates rank-and-file influence and may understate concentrated chair-level capture.

DISTINGUISHING TESTDo bill outcomes correlate more strongly with chair donor profiles than with aggregated committee membership profiles?

What this piece graphs rather than alleges.

Donor-committee correlation is structural; it is not by itself proof of corruption. The graph is a map for accountability journalism to operate against, not a verdict.

Permanent caveat

Constituent vs. capture

An industry-funded legislator from a district full of that industry's workers may be representing voter preference. The graph does not, by itself, distinguish.

Out of scope here

Individual scandal

This piece is structural. Specific-actor allegations require shoe-leather investigation that the graph helps target, not replace.
