ROOM 04 · THE FLAWS CARRY THE WISDOM

Keep only the good parts, and you lose the advice.

Grieving people already sanctify the dead. A classic study named it “husband sanctification,” and a later one found it in 7 of 10 widowers who did not remarry. FACT Bennett, Arnott and Soulsby, 2013 A griefbot can do it automatically and at scale.

In Infera's conversation with a grief counselor, the counterargument came from life: an aunt with a shoplifting compulsion whose history shaped the risks she understood and the advice people came to her for. “If you miss a piece, we don't know the butterfly effect.” Slide from as she was to as we wish, and watch where each tradition stops you.

BOTH SIDES · AND THE LIMITS

What each side can fairly claim.

The case for the business

  • Grief is already a market: urns, florists, counselors by the hour. One founder makes exactly this point and says regulation is needed. REPORTED
  • Recording a legacy before death, like the Dadbot or HereAfter's interviews, puts the person in charge of their own voice.
  • Some families need a gentler memory: survivors of a harsh parent, or young children. Jewish eulogy law itself allows slight warmth. INFERENCE
  • Playful AI with living elders brings real joy, and older Americans' use of AI doubled from 9% to 18% in a year. FACT AARP

The case for limits

  • A recurring bill plus engagement metrics turns grief into retention. The goodbye research shows the tactics can be tuned. FACT
  • Records, DNA and obituary scrapers can grow a dead person's profile without anyone's consent. AI-written obituaries of one person appeared on six sites within 48 hours. REPORTED
  • Every model update is a small edit to the dead, and getting the old version back has already been sold as a paid feature. REPORTED
  • No tradition here permits inventing a better person, which is what a sanitizing system does. INFERENCE
  • Limits. The firewood button is a scenario, not a documented product. The goodbye tactics were measured in general companion apps, not griefbots. The changelog's person is fictional; each update is mapped to a real practice, not to one company doing all of them.
