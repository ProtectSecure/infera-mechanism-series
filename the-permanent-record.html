    The Permanent Record.

    A church and its school can know a person from the baptismal font to the alumni office. Grow one up, and watch the file.

    Picture a life lived entirely inside one faith community: baptized in the parish, dropped off at the church nursery, educated in the church's school from kindergarten through a church college, married at the altar, giving every week for decades. Every one of those steps leaves a record. In the age of artificial intelligence, records that once sat in separate filing cabinets can be joined into one profile and used to predict things about the person.

    This exhibit does not allege that any church or school builds such a profile. It shows what can be collected, which laws reach it, what it could predict, and why a congregation might want it anyway.

      Room 2 · What the file can predict

      Join the drawers, and the file starts guessing.

      A file is a list of what happened. A model turns the list into predictions about what will happen. This is not hypothetical in religious life. A data company called Gloo has worked with more than 30,000 churches, sending some of them neighborhood "risk reports" estimating how many marriages nearby might be near divorce and how many people were at risk of addiction. REPORTEDChristian Post A consultant to nonprofits described what these systems want: to "predict your capacity to give, your likelihood of dropping out of a program," and compared it to banking.       Room 5 · Older than the state

      The permanent record was the church's idea first.

      1563

      The Council of Trent requires Catholic parishes to record baptisms and marriages. REPORTEDPolish Genealogical Society of America For centuries in much of Europe, the parish register was the record that a person had been born and married at all.

      1974

      The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act gives parents and students rights over school records, but only at schools that take federal Department of Education money. REPORTED

      2018

      Europe's data protection law treats religious belief as a special category of data. FACTGDPR Art. 9, as documented in HIPAA for the Pews

      2026

      A Belgian case asking whether a person can have a baptism erased from a parish register is before the EU's highest court. REPORTEDHIPAA for the Pews Many traditions hold that a sacrament cannot be undone.

      The through lineThe register was built to remember a soul permanently. Privacy law was built so people can control what is remembered about them. A lifelong digital file puts those two promises in the same drawer. INFERENCE
      Room 6 · Why a congregation keeps it

      The file is also care.

      The case for keeping it

      • Pastoral care depends on memory: knowing who is sick, grieving or new.
      • Children's check-in exists to make sure a child goes home with the right adult.
      • Need-based tuition aid is fairer when it is based on real income.
      • Many traditions consider sacramental records permanent by their nature, and a church's right to keep its own records is protected by religious liberty law.
      • Most congregations have small staffs and use these tools to do more with less.

      The case for limits

      • Records gathered for care can be reused for fundraising, or for outreach the member never agreed to.
      • A child cannot consent to a file that will follow them for life.
      • A breach exposes the most sensitive things a person ever said to their faith community.
      • Members who leave a faith may have no way to have their records removed.
      • Joining records and predicting from them changes what the file is, even if no new record is collected.

      Commitments Infera states across the Faith Layer: consent, truthful representation, limited collection, meaningful endings.
