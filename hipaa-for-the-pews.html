HIPAA for the Pews

The church already keeps a book of the names of the dead. It has no rule for the rest of what it keeps.

First, the name. HIPAA, the federal health privacy law, does not cover most congregations, and nothing here is legal advice. We borrow the name only because people recognize it. What a church holds about its people, from prayer requests to a child's check-in, mostly sits outside every privacy law there is. Four rooms show where the law stops, who can still reach the file, and where a church's own beliefs collide with a right to be erased. Then your choices across the Faith Layer become one charter.

    BOTH SIDES, AT THEIR STRONGEST

    Should congregations adopt a written data standard of their own?

    Yes: the law has a hole where the most intimate data sits

    • Prayer requests about illness, counseling notes and children's records usually fall outside HIPAA, COPPA, FERPA and every state privacy threshold; breach laws act only after the damage. FACT INFERENCE
    • Religious data is a target: an intrusion at the Latter-day Saints church that law enforcement linked to a state-sponsored campaign, and member records reportedly taken from two Seoul megachurches in 2026. REPORTED
    • Courts already reach church files. Holding less, on a schedule, is the only shield a church fully controls. FACT
    • Churches have done this on their own terms before: Germany's Catholic and Protestant data laws, and Church of England parish guidance that treats listing members in a directory as needing opt-in consent. FACT REPORTED
    • A standard a congregation writes itself is the best defense against one written for it. INFERENCE

    No, or not this way: who writes the rules for a church?

    • Any outside standard, from a vendor, an insurer, a regulator or a journalism project, invites outsiders to define what a church may record about souls. A federal judge blocked Washington's attempt to require priests to report what they hear in confession. REPORTED
    • A "voluntary" standard can harden into an insurer requirement or evidence of negligence in court; one church-law publication makes the same argument about children's privacy rules. REPORTED
    • Erasure rights collide with sacraments a church holds permanent, as Room D shows.
    • Most congregations are small and volunteer-run. A formal standard could push them onto vendor platforms owned by private equity, concentrating the risk. INFERENCE
    • A badge without an audit can make members trust a church more than its security deserves, and a public list of who complies could become a target map for communities with a history of being watched. INFERENCE
    • What this exhibit did not prove. Nothing here is legal advice. Room A's placements are our reading of the definitions; edge cases such as denominational health plans and parish nurses turn on facts we cannot see.
    • Several incidents in Room C rest on secondary reporting or a researcher's or company's account, as labeled. We did not survey denominational rules, Islamic or Jewish congregational data guidance, or any US diocesan data policy, and we found no case of a church member list sold in bankruptcy.
    • The Belgian baptism case awaits a ruling from the EU Court of Justice; an Advocate General's opinion is advice to the court, not a decision.
    • The charter below is a tool for conversation. It binds no one until a congregation's own people adopt it.

    Our congregation's data charter · draft

      FORM YOUR OWN READ

      Five questions for a church council.

      1. What do we keep about our members that no law requires us to protect?
      2. If a court asked for everything tomorrow, what would we hand over, and could we have kept less?
      3. Which apps and vendors do we recommend, and what happens to their data if they are sold or fail?
      4. Which of our records do we believe are permanent, and what would we say to someone who asks to leave?
      5. Who in our congregation decides, and how would every member know what we decided?
