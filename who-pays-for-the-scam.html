    Who pays for the scam?

    If someone steals your card, the law limits what you lose. If someone tricks you into sending the money yourself, in the United States you usually lose it all. Britain changed that rule in 2024. Here is what happened next.

    About 13 minutesNo sliders · eight roomsNot financial or legal advice
    The short version
    1. U.S. law protects you when a payment is unauthorized. If you were deceived into making the payment yourself, banks are generally not required to repay you. REPORTEDGAO, 2024
    2. Since 7 October 2024, British banks must repay most victims of these scams, splitting the cost between the sending and receiving banks. REPORTED
    3. An independent review found reimbursement rose and scam losses fell by about £73 million a year. REPORTED That suggests a bank that pays for the harm has a reason to build the second alarm. INFERENCE

    Room 2 · The two-alarm panel

    One customer. Two doors. One alarm rings.

    Same $2,000, gone from the same account. Press each dial to see whether U.S. law locks the loss away from you.

    A thief sent it

    Stolen card or login · "unauthorized"

    Waiting
    Regulation E defines an unauthorized transfer as one started by someone else, without your authority. Report a lost card or login within two business days and your liability is capped at $50; later, generally $500. REPORTED12 CFR 1005.6

    You sent it

    Tricked into pressing send · "authorized"

    Waiting
    Because you started the transfer, banks argue it is not "unauthorized," and Regulation E's protection does not apply. Banks and payment apps are generally not required to repay you. REPORTEDGAO, July 2024; Atlanta Fed Some do anyway: since mid-2023, banks on Zelle have refunded some scams where the caller pretended to be a bank, a government agency or an existing service provider. REPORTEDReuters, Nov 2023

    Room 6 · Who paid before

    Every alarm has a bill behind it.

    Offered as a comparison, not a prediction: the dates where the law moved the loss, and what followed.

    1970
    Credit cards: $50

    Congress capped what a cardholder owes for unauthorized use at $50 and put the burden of proof on the card issuer. REPORTED15 U.S.C. 1643, Pub. L. 91-508

    1978
    Debit and transfers

    The Electronic Fund Transfer Act, carried out through Regulation E, set the $50 and $500 limits for unauthorized electronic transfers. REPORTED

    2023
    Zelle, under pressure

    Banks on Zelle began refunding some impostor scams, after Washington pressure, as of 30 June. REPORTEDReuters

    2024
    Britain moves the loss

    Mandatory reimbursement from 7 October, up to £85,000 a claim, split 50:50 between sending and receiving banks, claims within 13 months. The cap was cut from an earlier £415,000. REPORTEDPSR PS24/7; PwC

    2025
    A U.S. suit, dropped

    The CFPB dropped its Zelle lawsuit on 4 March, among several dismissals under the new administration. REPORTEDEngadget

    2026
    Two plans in Congress

    House Financial Services Republicans' July report backs more fraud tools for banks but no shift of scam losses; committee Democrats back the Protecting Consumers from Payment Scams Act, which would split losses among the institutions involved. REPORTEDAmerican Banker, 24 Jul 2026

    The patternCard fraud detection grew up under a rule that put the loss on the issuer. Britain's scam detection improved after a rule put the loss on the banks. INFERENCE The thesis of this series: where the law makes the company pay for the harm, the second alarm gets built. INFERENCE

    Room 7 · Coach's challenge

    Throw the flag.

    Every argument here has a real answer from the other side. Tap a flag to hear it.

    The banks' strongest worry is moral hazard: if you will be repaid, why be careful? The British rule answers with a gross-negligence exception, and the July 2026 review found no evidence of reckless consumer behaviour. REPORTED
    True. The costs fall on banks, and a bank can pass its costs on to customers. INFERENCE The review estimated a short-term net benefit of £17 million to £29 million a year after counting those higher costs, and called the estimate conservative. REPORTED
    Many scams begin on a phone, a text or social media: texts were the most reported contact method in 2025, and social media led reported losses by contact method. REPORTEDFTC data via Detroit Free Press That leaves a fair question the British rule does not answer: whether the platforms where scams start should share the cost. INFERENCE
    To catch a scam before you send, a bank has to watch how you pay and may slow or question your payments. That protection costs some privacy and some freedom to move your own money. INFERENCE This is the series' standing mirror.
