Checked in. Never checked out.

Every Sunday, a church check-in system prints a sticker for a child and saves a record. The sticker is in the trash by lunch. In the systems we read about, the record has no end date. This exhibit shows what a child's file can hold, who can read it, and how long it outlives the class.

        BOTH SIDES, AT THEIR STRONGEST

        Is a church's children's file a shield or a liability?

        A shield: the data exists to protect children

        • Check-in systems were built to stop the wrong adult from walking out with a child. Photos and matching codes are how a volunteer who has never met a family tells a parent from a stranger.
        • An allergy on the sticker can save a life when a volunteer hands out snacks. A medical note on paper in a binder cannot be seen in time.
        • Not Authorized lists exist because custody orders are real and volunteers need to know.
        • Attendance history helps a church answer the hardest question after an allegation: who was in the room, and when.

        A liability: the file outlives the reason

        • A sticker broadcasts a child's health to anyone in a hallway, and the record of it is never thrown away.
        • The systems we read keep history by design. A child who left at 12 can still be a row in a database at 30.
        • The most sensitive facts, like custody conflicts, sit in the same file as attendance, managed by volunteers, readable by whoever administers it next.
        • The federal children's privacy law generally does not reach noncommercial churches, so protection depends on each church's habits and each vendor's contract.
        WHAT THIS EXHIBIT DID NOT PROVE

        We read two vendors' public help pages; we did not test their software or read their contracts, and other vendors may work differently. We found no reported breach of a church check-in system; the 140,000-record exposure was a childcare platform.

        The Churchix figure is from 2015 and the vendor's own count. We found no current data on face recognition in US children's ministry.

        Whether Illinois's biometric law reaches a church is our reading of its broad definition of a private entity, not a court ruling. Washington's 2017 biometric law is noted but not summarized. Nothing here is legal advice.

        Retention counts in Room B are a model built from the sliders, not records from any church.

        FORM YOUR OWN READ

        Five questions for parents at the check-in desk

        1. What prints on my child's sticker, and who can read it in the hallway?
        2. Who can see the Not Authorized list and the medical notes, and are they volunteers?
        3. When my child ages out or we leave, what happens to the record, and can I ask for it to be deleted?
        4. Which company stores this, and what does its contract with the church say about breaches and deletion?
        5. Is anyone's face, fingerprint or photo being matched by software, and did I sign anything saying so?
