Who Holds the Speedometer

States have found a new answer to drivers who hit 100 mph: a GPS box that will not let the car go faster than the posted limit. It keeps people driving who would otherwise lose their license. It also writes a log of every trip, and in most places the law does not say who may read it.

01How it works

Speeding's version of the breathalyzer lock

An ignition interlock makes a drunk-driving offender blow clean before the engine starts. Intelligent speed assistance does the same for speed. A box reads the car's position by GPS, looks up the limit in a map database and cuts engine power as the car reaches it, even with the pedal down. FACT Virginia's devices do not brake, so a downhill run can still carry the car over the limit. REPORTED

The device replaces a punishment. In Virginia, a judge can order it instead of a 60-day to six-month suspension for reckless driving or racing, and drivers with too many demerit points can choose it instead of a 90-day suspension. FACT Defense lawyers like that clients can still go to the grocery store. A public defender warns it becomes the new default: "he can drive, he just has to do this program." REPORTED Brianna Cox, via The Wall Street Journal.

02The override question

How much control do you keep?

Every program lets the driver go over the limit briefly, to merge or escape trouble. How often is the real policy choice. Pick a rulebook, then press the override.

03When the map is wrong

GPS drift

Virginia's first months brought glitches. One driver's car was cut to 35 mph on a highway, forcing him to pull over, his lawyer said. Another device applied a cross street's limit at an intersection. Officials and a supplier blame "GPS drift," when the device picks up a lower limit from a nearby road, and say a fix is coming. REPORTED

04Sixteen tickets to a governor

New York lets the camera decide

Most states trigger a device through a court conviction or license points. New York's 2026 law, written for New York City, triggers it from school-zone speed-camera tickets, civil violations mailed to the car's owner. Red-light tickets do not count. The original bill would have reached about 150,000 drivers; the final law reaches fewer than 18,000. FACT

The device goes on every car registered in the driver's name, and the state can refuse registration changes meant to dodge it. Refusing to install can cost the registration, and driving unregistered is a misdemeanor. City, state and taxi-commission vehicles, and commercial vehicles shared by two or more drivers, are exempt. Drivers can contest the order in court. FACT The city program still needs City Council approval, and the transportation commissioner has not committed to a start date. REPORTED

05Who holds the speedometer

Every trip leaves a log

The device has to know where the car is to know the limit, so every program produces a location and speed record. The laws differ most on what happens to it. A national legislative analyst told the Journal that privacy guardrails control access. That is true in some states and unwritten in others, including Virginia, the one state collecting this data at scale. INFERENCE

08Both sides

A tool that works, or a box with no rules?

For the devices

  • A small group of drivers does outsized harm; a New York City study found drivers with 10 camera tickets were in deadly crashes at nearly three times the general rate. REPORTED
  • Up to 75% of drivers with suspended licenses keep driving anyway; a device controls the car they actually drive. REPORTED
  • In a federal study of New York City's own fleet, time spent at more than 11 mph over the limit fell 64%. FACT
  • Support crosses parties, from a Democratic delegate in Virginia to a Republican governor who signed it. REPORTED

Against, or wary

  • The fleet study measured city employees, not convicted offenders; no offender-program results are published yet. INFERENCE
  • Costs fall hardest on low-income drivers, and some bills lacked data limits, judicial discretion or an emergency override, the ACLU of Rhode Island testified. REPORTED
  • New York converts machine-issued civil tickets into a hardware order; lawmakers raised due process concerns. REPORTED
  • A device can misread the road, as Virginia's GPS drift cases show, and in Virginia the statute is silent on who sees the logs. FACT
