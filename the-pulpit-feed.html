    BOTH SIDES, AT THEIR STRONGEST

    Is religious speech treated unfairly online? It depends who you ask, and both have a record.

    The religious-liberty case

    • A 2023 FBI field-office memo labeled some Catholics as potential extremists and floated sources in parishes. The bureau retracted it; an FBI internal review found it created the appearance of investigating belief, while the Justice Department's inspector general found no evidence of bias. FACT
    • Alphabet told Congress in 2025 that senior Biden administration officials repeatedly pressed YouTube to remove pandemic content that did not break its rules. REPORTED
    • Rules built around gender and sexuality have swept in religious satire and teaching, and Meta itself estimated 10 to 20% of its removals may be mistakes. FACT

    The minority-faith case

    • The best-documented over-enforcement hit Arabic speakers and Muslims, found by an audit Meta commissioned; "shaheed" was the single most-removed word on its platforms. FACT
    • Under-enforcement hurts too: the Oversight Board found Meta wrongly left up calls to smash mosques during the 2024 UK riots. FACT
    • Political targeting of worshippers is mainstream: a group geofenced Mass-goers and matched them to voter files in 2020. REPORTEDthe group's own claim

    What both can agree on

    • We found no platform that publishes error rates by religion or by language, so every side argues from anecdotes. INFERENCEsearch result
    • Ranking, not belief, drives reach. The biggest "Christian" pages in 2019 were troll farms, and a fake pope outdrew the real one.
    • Moderation is moving to AI, and we found no public audit of how AI moderators treat religious speech outside English. INFERENCEsearch result
    • What this exhibit did not prove. Room A is our model of ranking, not any platform's code; the 2019 troll-farm figures come from a leaked internal report as described by MIT Technology Review.
    • The nine cases in Room B are not a sample. They are the best-documented cases we found in each direction, and they cannot show which faith is treated worse. That is the point of the room.
    • The pastor survey was produced with Gloo, a company that sells church technology. The fake-pope view counts come from an AFP review of channels, not platform data.
    FORM YOUR OWN READ

    Five questions for a congregation that posts.

    1. When our sermon is posted, who are we hoping hears it, and do we know who actually does?
    2. Which pages in our members' feeds claim our faith, and who runs them?
    3. If our post is removed, who in our congregation knows how to appeal, and in which language?
    4. Has AI helped write anything said from our pulpit, and did we say so?
    5. Would we ever play a voice that is not the speaker's own, and who decides?
