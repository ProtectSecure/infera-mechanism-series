Babel or Pentecost

Whose words are understood when a machine translates the faith?

In Genesis, one language builds a tower and is scattered. At Pentecost, in Acts, many languages stay and each listener hears in their own tongue. The title borrows that Jewish and Christian pair because Pope Leo XIV made Babel, set against the rebuilding of Jerusalem, one of the two central images of his 2026 encyclical on AI, released on Pentecost Monday. Other traditions frame language differently, and this exhibit gives them their own room.

What this showsThree benchmarks built from different faith perspectives all found that general models default to secular framing. FACTpreprints The authors of the study above frame it as "recognition, not capability": counsel improved sharply when the model was told the user's tradition and given a guide to it.

We report findings, not model rankings. Several of these studies rank the model that helped compile this page favorably, so we leave the rankings out. What this showsStudies find the same flattening in different places: a company benchmark found models swap specific Christian terms for generic spiritual language FACTcompany benchmark; native-speaker raters scored idioms and puns lowest across 15 languages FACTpreprint; and a survey of Islamic chatbots warns they can collapse legitimate disagreement among jurists into one answer and blend Sunni and Shia positions without saying so FACTpreprint.
REPORTED

Recited, not translated

Most Sunni schools of law hold that the opening chapter of the Quran must be recited in Arabic in prayer, and scholars describe translations as explanations of the meaning rather than the Quran itself. The Hanafi school allows a translation for those who cannot yet speak Arabic. For this tradition, the untranslated word is part of the worship.

REPORTED

Translated on its own terms

Te Hiku Media built Māori speech recognition and released its data under a license that restricts use to the benefit of Māori people. Here a community chooses whether and how to be translated, rather than refusing translation.

Where Catholic teaching draws it

A 2025 Vatican note says "the presumption of substituting God for an artifact of human making is idolatry." Pope Leo XIV's 2026 encyclical names a "Babel syndrome": the pretense that one language, "even a digital one," can translate "the mystery of the person" into data.

REPORTED

A different tradition's image

At Kōdai-ji in Kyoto, a Zen temple, an android of Kannon, the bodhisattva of compassion, has delivered a sermon on the Heart Sutra since 2019. It is not powered by AI. A temple steward said it "will never die." A Chabad-affiliated rabbi warned in 2026 that attributing a soul to AI risks idolatry.

BOTH SIDES, AT THEIR STRONGEST

Should faith communities welcome machine translation and AI guides? Pentecost, or Babel?

Pentecost: welcome it, carefully

  • Languages judged to need a translation that has not begun fell from 985 to 544 in one year, partly because need was reassessed, and AI drafting is used in nearly 500 projects, with translators treating output as "draft 0." FACT
  • Live translation now works in earbuds and video calls in about 9 to 13 languages, enough to greet many newcomers in their own tongue. FACT REPORTED
  • The secular default can be fixed: naming the tradition and giving the model a guide raised alignment sharply. FACTpreprint
  • Tradition-built tools exist, from a Catholic library of about 27,000 Church documents to a Jewish text assistant that says it is not a legal authority. REPORTED

Babel: slow down

  • One model family studied represents meaning closer to English in its middle layers, whatever the input. FACTone study
  • Untranslatable terms flatten to generic words, and disagreement within a tradition can be collapsed into one answer. FACTpreprints
  • Single-faith AI products may split communities into separate, unexamined answer machines. REPORTED
  • Catholic and Jewish voices independently use the word idolatry for treating AI as a being or a god. FACT REPORTED

What both can agree on

  • A model should follow the tradition a user names, and hold it when pushed. That conclusion came from a study co-authored by researchers from Islamic, Catholic and secular institutions.
  • Humans keep final authority over scripture and counsel; translators themselves call machine output a first draft.
  • No study we found shows that machine translation increases understanding between religions. It is a hope, not a finding. INFERENCEsearch result
  • What this exhibit did not prove. We did not run our own tests of models. Room B reports one preprint's findings on five models; other models and later versions may differ.
  • The word glosses in Room C are simplified. Scholars within each tradition dispute their boundaries, and "dharma" means different things in Hindu, Buddhist, Jain and Sikh usage.
  • The Islamic position in Room C summarizes one published fatwa's account of the schools of law; it is not a ruling, and practice varies.
  • Every case in Room D is from press reports; we did not use the products.
FORM YOUR OWN READ

Five questions for a congregation of many tongues.

  1. Which languages do our members pray in, and which do we serve them in?
  2. Who reads a machine translation before it is read aloud?
  3. When a member asks an AI about our faith, which faith does it assume?
  4. Which words of ours should never be flattened, and who decides?
  5. Would we let a machine speak as a saint, a prophet or God? Where is our line?
