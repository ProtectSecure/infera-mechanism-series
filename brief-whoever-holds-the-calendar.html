Whoever Holds the Calendar

Washington, D.C. is deciding whether driverless cars can carry paying riders. The fight looks like safety against jobs. Underneath, it is a fight over dates. A tech company that can scale fast wants a yes before the year ends. Opponents want the vote pushed to a new council and a new mayor. An anonymous group has spent millions on ads. And Congress is writing a federal rule that could make the city's choices smaller. This brief is about how money, scale and speed are used to press on a process built to move slowly, and who wins when the clock runs out.

01Move the vote

The date is the decision

A D.C. bill has to pass the full council twice, with at least 13 days between the two votes. Only two legislative meetings are left this year, on Dec. 1 and Dec. 15. So the bill has to clear Charles Allen's five-member transportation committee, then the Committee of the Whole, in time to be voted on Dec. 1. Pick when the committee votes and how it goes, and follow the bill to the street.

    03What the concessions bought

    Cheaper to enter, less for drivers

    Allen rewrote the bill on Nov. 10 to win votes. Some changes added protections: a right for rideshare drivers and AV maintenance workers to organize, a citywide cap, wheelchair-accessible cars.     05What the safety data can and cannot carry

    Fewer crashes, thin records

    Both sides cite safety. The best independent number so far comes from the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, which compared Waymo's driverless miles from 2021 to 2024 with human drivers in the same places: a crash involvement rate 68% lower overall. FACT By city:

    Phoenix76% lower
    Los Angeles71% lower
    San Francisco35% lower
    Austin4% higher

    The caveats are the institute's own. Federal crash reports do not require mileage, so only Waymo's self-reported miles were available; the data predate recent changes; and Austin's sample is small. The researchers warned that weak data collection makes a wider rollout hard to monitor. REPORTED

    Other concerns are about the city, not the average. Councilmember Zachary Parker cited firefighters' reports that driverless cars do not move for emergency vehicles. REPORTED NBC4 reported the revised bill responds to viral videos of driverless cars stopping in traffic during a power outage by requiring backup plans. REPORTED On the other side, writer Andy Masley points to about 40 traffic deaths a year in D.C., concentrated in lower-income wards. REPORTED

    A safety record measured in other cities' streets is the kind of evidence that arrives slower than the product. That gap is what both the delay and the deadline are arguing about. INFERENCE

    06Infera read

    The pacing problem has two directions

    Scholars call the gap between how fast technology moves and how fast law adapts "the pacing problem." The usual story has companies outrunning rules. D.C. shows the rest of it. INFERENCE

    Money

    Paid to slow it

    The biggest visible spender is on the side of waiting: an ad group that will not say who funds it. Allen calls it "non-transparent, out-of-town money." REPORTED Punchbowl News described the group as having union ties. REPORTED

    Limit: we could not confirm who funds CARS. WATCH

    Scale

    Built to the cap

    Waymo says it runs fully driverless rides in at least 14 U.S. cities, has three D.C. facilities and has promised hundreds of local jobs. NBC4 reported it appears to be the only company ready to operate at the bill's volume. REPORTED

    Limit: a cap written around the one ready company sets the market's first shape. INFERENCE

    Speed

    Two deadlines, pointing opposite ways

    The company and the chair want a vote before Dec. 15. The mayor-elect and two committee members want it after the new council is seated. Each side's timing serves its position. INFERENCE

    Limit: a delay is not a no. Parker says he supports authorizing AVs and wants "a few more months." REPORTED

    Above the city

    The clock nobody in D.C. controls

    Congress can review any D.C. act, and a federal AV law, if one passes, could limit what cities may require of the cars. The slower the city, the more room for a higher level to decide first. INFERENCE

    Limit: the federal bill has not passed the House. WATCH

    07Both sides

    Set the terms now, or wait for the new council?

    The case for passing it this year

    • Allen calls it "the most worker-friendly, safety-focused, most-progressive legislation" on driverless cars in the country, and asks what waiting would change after three hearings in two and a half years: "For What?" REPORTED
    • Henderson, on the committee, says she will vote yes, calling the cap "controlled growth that won't change the status quo overnight for labor." REPORTED
    • Waymo's spokesperson said the company applauds Allen "after nearly 15 years of D.C. studying this technology," and says the bill will "create safer roads, high-quality jobs, and a more connected city." REPORTED
    • In the IIHS study, Waymo's driverless cars had 68% fewer crashes per mile than human drivers overall across four cities, though not in Austin, where a small sample showed 4% more. FACT
    • If the city does not set its own terms, someone else may: the companies keep testing, and a federal rule could arrive first. INFERENCE

    The other side: the case for waiting

    • Mayor-elect Janeese Lewis George says the bill is being rushed and should be taken up next year, especially on job losses. REPORTED
    • Councilmember Elissa Silverman wants to act once the new transportation, for-hire vehicle and employment directors are in place. REPORTED
    • An SEIU 32BJ spokesperson said the midterm results show "widespread public distrust against corporate power and artificial intelligence." The Teamsters and the D.C. Democratic Socialists also oppose moving now. REPORTED
    • Uber questioned the timing, saying the council should consider "how these proposals work together," even as Uber carries Waymo cars in Austin and Atlanta. REPORTED
    • The driver training share went from half to a quarter of a per-mile charge that was itself halved, so training money per mile fell by three quarters, and it ends after three years, while the cars would not reach the street until late 2027 at the earliest. INFERENCE

    What nobody outside can see yet: the final text of the revised bill on the council's site, who funds CARS, whether the committee votes this week at all, and whether the council adds a meeting in December. WATCH
