The List
at the Door

In Britain, shoppers wrongly flagged by store face-scanning cameras are being walked out of shops and left to clear their own names. The same kind of system runs in American stores. Whether anything protects you depends on which side of a state line you are standing on.

~300,000"Known repeat offender" alerts sent to UK retailers by Facewatch in the first half of 2026, per the company REPORTED
21Innocent people wrongly flagged by UK shops who contacted Big Brother Watch in a year REPORTED
10+Publicly reported false arrests in the U.S. from face recognition errors, nearly all of Black people (ACLU) REPORTED
0Federal laws written for private-sector face recognition. The FTC acts only against unfair or deceptive practices FACT

The 97% ticket is an illustration. The number echoes a "97 percent match" a Michigan skating rink cited when it ejected a misidentified teen, which the ACLU called a gross misrepresentation. REPORTED

01What happened in Britain

Flagged at the till, left to prove a negative

A man walked into a Home Bargains for vitamins and was ordered to leave. His wife says he now feels "isolated" and is "scared to go into a shop." At a Budgens, a woman was removed for supposedly stealing "loads of meat" from a store the couple had not visited in months. Neither got a chance to dispute the match on the spot. REPORTED TechRadar, via Futurism, Nov. 1, 2026. Neither outlet reported a response from the retailers.

In May, The Guardian reported a whistleblower, a former security guard at a shop using Facewatch, who said innocent people had been put on watchlists "for malicious reasons." Big Brother Watch said it had heard from 21 wrongly flagged people in the past year. REPORTED

The systems are spreading anyway. Facewatch says it serves more than 100 businesses, including Sainsbury's, B&M and Spar. Sainsbury's plans to go from 55 stores to more than 200 by the end of 2026. This autumn, Facewatch says, its network will alert police in an average of four seconds when one of its "worst offenders" is matched. REPORTED The Guardian, July 10, 2026.

A British shopper does have one universal tool: a subject access request. Under UK data protection law, a company must hand over the data it holds on you, usually for free, within one month. FACT ICO guidance. Shoppers told The Guardian the process is slow and asks them to hand over even more personal information. REPORTED

02The American versions

It already happened here

American cases come in two kinds. Sometimes a store's own system flags a customer. Sometimes police run a face search and treat the match as an identification. Some cases mix both: a store's loss-prevention team runs the match, then hands the name to police.

Store systemPolice search
Rite Aid · nationwide2012 to 2020

Thousands of false alarms

The FTC found Rite Aid's system threw thousands of false matches. It sometimes matched shoppers to people accused at stores thousands of miles away. Black, Asian and female customers were more likely to be misidentified. Employees publicly accused customers, sometimes in front of family. In December 2023 the FTC banned Rite Aid from using face recognition for five years, without a fine. FACT The chain closed its last stores in early October 2025 after its second bankruptcy, so the ban now binds a company with no stores. REPORTED ABC7.

Houston · Sunglass Hut, Macy's2022

Harvey Murphy Jr.

His lawsuit says EssilorLuxottica's loss-prevention chief, working with Macy's, matched robbery footage to Murphy's 1980s mugshots. Murphy says he was in Sacramento, about 2,000 miles away. He was jailed and says he was sexually assaulted there before charges were dropped. He sued for $10 million in January 2024. Macy's declined to comment. REPORTED allegations in the suit

Detroit · watch theft2020

Robert Williams

He was arrested at his home in front of his wife and two daughters after a false match. His 2024 settlement bars Detroit police from arresting anyone on a face match alone, or on a photo lineup that directly follows one. It also ordered an audit of every face recognition warrant since 2017. FACT The rules show up in the numbers: violent-crime face searches fell from about 100 in 2023 to 9 in 2025, and one produced a lead. All nine targeted Black men, the audit's findings are unreleased, and Detroit can still route searches through the Michigan State Police. REPORTED BridgeDetroit, from police data.

Detroit · carjackingFeb. 2023

Porcha Woodruff

Eight months pregnant, she was arrested while getting her children ready for school and held 10 hours. Police later admitted she was the wrong person. On Aug. 5, 2025, a federal judge dismissed her suit, finding the officer had probable cause. Her lawyer said an appeal is planned. FACT

St. Louis · transit assault2021 to 2024

Christopher Gatlin

A grainy, hooded image taken from above returned his mugshot as a possible match. He spent about 17 months in jail, unable to pay a $75,000 bond, before charges were dismissed in March 2024. His suit survived a motion to dismiss. In August 2026 he added Amazon Web Services, naming Rekognition as the matching tool. AWS says it will investigate. REPORTED

Wegmans · NYC and moreJan. 2026

The signs went up

New York City's disclosure law brought Wegmans' entrance notices to light. Wegmans says it scans faces only at some "elevated risk" stores, to flag people previously caught for theft or threats. Some names come from law enforcement, "case-by-case." It will not say how long it keeps images or how someone gets off the list. REPORTED

A Washington Post review found at least eight Americans wrongly arrested after face recognition matches, all eventually cleared. It found 15 departments in 12 states had arrested people on AI matches without independent evidence. In six of the eight wrongful cases, officers never checked the alibi. REPORTED Jan. 2025. The ACLU lists Lowe's, Madison Square Garden and, apparently, Macy's in New York City among other retail users. REPORTED

03Where American law stands

Pick where you're standing

No federal law governs a store scanning your face. What exists is a patchwork of state laws and city ordinances. Pick a place to see the rule at its door. The meter shows how much the rule lets you, the shopper, do on your own.

04Where we differ

Britain has one rulebook. America has dozens.

The British shoppers have it hard, but they start with a right that most Americans lack: a nationwide law that covers face data and a regulator to complain to.

05If it happens to you

What to do, in order

At the door

  1. Ask plainly: "Did a camera system flag me? What do you think I did, and on what date?" Write down the answers.
  2. Stay calm. Don't argue the match, and don't sign anything.
  3. Get names: the employee, the manager, the store number, the time. Photograph any biometric notice at the entrance. New York City and Connecticut require them.
  4. Lock down your alibi that day: phone location history, card receipts, transit records for the date they cite. Murphy's alibi put him 2,000 miles away.

If police get involved

  1. Ask for a lawyer, and say little until you have one.
  2. Have your lawyer ask whether face recognition produced the lead. Maryland law makes prosecutors disclose it. In New Jersey, the state Supreme Court ruled on June 24, 2026, in State v. Miles, that prosecutors must name the software and its maker, share its public error rates and turn over the photo used in the search. Detroit bars arrests on a match alone. FACT
  3. Push for your alibi to be checked. In most wrongful cases the Post reviewed, no one checked it. REPORTED

Afterward

  1. Write to the company. Ask whether you are on a watchlist, who added you and why, who else sees the list, and how to get off it. Keep copies.
  2. Use your state's privacy rights if you have them: access and deletion requests. In Maryland, an access request is free once a year.
  3. Complain to your state attorney general and to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov. In Illinois, ask a lawyer about BIPA.
  4. If you were arrested, ask about sealing or expunging the record, and check background reports for errors.
  5. Ask a lawyer about the claims others have filed: false imprisonment, malicious prosecution, negligence.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and change often. For your own situation, talk to a lawyer, a legal aid office or your state's ACLU affiliate.

06How it can affect your life

A false match travels

1

Locked out of the stores near you. A watchlist entry can cover every store in a chain. In Britain it can cover a whole subscriber network. If the chain is your pharmacy or your nearest grocery, the ban takes those too. INFERENCE

2

Accused in public. The FTC found Rite Aid workers accused customers in front of friends and family. FACT

3

Jail time. Gatlin spent about 17 months in jail. Woodruff was held 10 hours while eight months pregnant. Murphy says he was assaulted before release. REPORTED

4

A record that outlives the mistake. An arrest can appear on background checks for jobs and housing after charges are dropped, unless it is sealed. INFERENCE

5

Money and time. Clearing your name means letters, lawyers and court dates. Gatlin's took more than two years. REPORTED

6

Fear. The British husband in the Home Bargains case is "scared to go into a shop." REPORTED

The errors do not fall evenly

NIST tested 189 algorithms from 99 developers in 2019. Most produced more false positives for Asian and African American faces than for white faces. For many algorithms the gap was 10 to 100 times. Higher false-positive rates for African American women showed up in one-to-many searches, the kind a watchlist runs. The most accurate algorithms were also among the most even-handed. FACT
