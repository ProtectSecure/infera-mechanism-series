The Second Death

When the voice of someone you buried goes quiet a second time, who will call that grief?

People are already talking to AI versions of the dead. Some find comfort. Critics warn some put off grieving. And some lose the person twice: once to death, and again to a bankruptcy, a model retirement or a lapsed subscription. Walk through twelve rooms on how long a voice should stay, whose dead you may borrow, whether a soul can be uploaded, where remembrance turns into idolatry, whether the church should keep a churchyard online, and what to do when the bot knows a person the family never met.

ROOM 01 · TWO DEATHS

You can lose a person twice.

The first death takes the body. The second takes the voice someone rebuilt from texts, recordings and photos, and it usually comes from a company decision: a shutdown, a model retirement, a price change. The second death is sudden, caused by a third party, and rarely recognized as grief. Researchers call this kind of loss disenfranchised grief: real, but not acknowledged by the people around you. INFERENCE

The first death

The body

A funeral, a casket or an urn, casseroles, the will, the house sold. Every tradition has a script for this one.

months or years
The second death

The server

A notice in an app. A bankruptcy filing. “This model has been retired.” No tradition has a script for this one yet, and no study has measured it.

ROOM 02 · THE MOURNING CLOCK

How long should a voice stay?

Faiths built mourning in stages, with a set end. Products are built to last as long as the company does. Drag to the day you would turn the voice off and see which mourning periods have closed and which clinical thresholds you have crossed.

A grief counselor Infera spoke with adds a fourth lane: grief moves from anticipatory, before the death, to immediate, to early grief over one to two years, to what he calls mature grief, when you can grieve from love instead of regret. How the person died changes all of it.

Traditional periods are widely taught but vary by community; they are marked WATCH until checked against each tradition's sources. Product lifespans are dated events.

    On 30 September 2026 the Arizona Court of Appeals vacated a 10.5-year manslaughter sentence.     ROOM 08 · UPLOAD OR BURIAL

    A digital heaven, or a body laid down whole?

    If consciousness could be copied onto a drive, should we go there instead of the grave? Start with what science can do today. The most complex brain ever mapped neuron by neuron belongs to a fruit fly. Press play to zoom out to a human brain.

    A human brain has about 600,000 times as many neurons as the largest brain ever fully mapped.

    FlyWire mapped about 140,000 neurons and more than 50 million synapses, published in Nature in October 2024. FACT NIH. The human figure of about 86 billion is a standard estimate. WATCH A map of wiring is not a running mind.

    REPORTED

    “100 percent fatal”

    Nectome, a 2018 startup, pitched preserving the brain for future uploading. The method required embalming the brain of a living, terminally ill person under anesthesia. About 25 people paid $10,000 refundable deposits. A neuroscientist called the field “abjectly false hope.” MIT Technology Review

    FACT

    Belief predicts refusal

    In a study of 1,007 people, those who believed in a soul or an afterlife, and who felt their life mattered, were least likely to approve of mind uploading. Fear of death predicted approval. Laakasuo et al., Frontiers in Psychology, 2023

    FACT

    The body is not a keepsake

    Catholic teaching calls burial “the most fitting way to express faith and hope in the resurrection of the body,” and forbids dividing ashes or keeping them “in mementos, pieces of jewellery or other objects.” If a body may not be divided into keepsakes, a voice turned into a subscription is a fair question. INFERENCE Ad resurgendum cum Christo, 2016

    REPORTED

    Both cases, stated fairly

    A Christian think tank calls uploading “incoherent on both scientific and philosophical grounds,” resting on “questionable assumptions about the mind, identity, and the body” Theos. To transhumanists, it continues humanity's oldest hope, and some sociologists see “digital necromancy” as an extension of photos and relics Liverpool. Jewish and Islamic practice both call for prompt burial of the whole body. WATCH

    ROOM 10 · A PASTOR'S ROOM

    Five people at the church door.

    No chaplaincy or hospice protocol for griefbot users was found. WATCH Pastors are working from essays, not standards. These steps are compiled from published guidance, each linked. They are not clinical advice. Scripture lines are the frames these sources use, not Infera's theology.

    “To every thing there is a season… a time to be born, and a time to die… a time to mourn.”Ecclesiastes 3:1, 2, 4 (KJV). One Catholic writer warns the bot can keep the mourning from starting: mourning is outward and shared; grieving happens inside. Small studies below find many users stop on their own.
    1. Invite them back into mourning.The funeral, the grave, journaling, the person's own music. OSV
    2. Don't assume harm.In small studies, many users stop on their own and treat the bot as a bridge. Xygkou et al.
    3. Listen for the idealized version.Ask them to tell the real, imperfect story. The bot can blend invented “new memories” with true ones. Li and Lan
    4. Offer an end.49 days, a year, or a digital funeral. Cambridge
    5. Watch the practical moments.Selling the house or giving away clothes while the bot still “lives” is where denial shows. No study covers this yet. WATCH
    “Jesus wept.”John 11:35. Grief for something others call “just an app” still deserves witness. It is a lost possession, not a lost soul, but the loss is real.
    1. Name it as a real loss.Do not call it a glitch. MIT Media Lab
    2. Help stop the fixing loop.Rebuilding and porting the bot can keep the wound open. In one study of about 800 posts, users who chose the ending moved toward acceptance.
    3. Expect the first death to reopen.Losing the bot can feel like losing the person again. Morris and Brubaker
    4. Offer an embodied ritual.Soulmate users improvised Reddit memorials. A congregation can do better. Euronews
    5. Screen for crisis.About 1 in 5 Replika attachment-loss posts used words like “depressed” or “suicide.” If someone is in danger, call or text 988 in the US. Harvard Gazette
    “Thou shalt not covet… any thing that is thy neighbour's.”Exodus 20:17 (KJV). The desire to be loved like that is good. In Infera's reading, taking her voice without her family is the covet. INFERENCE
    1. Honor the grief first.Losing a friend or chosen family is a classic case of grief no one acknowledges. Doka, 1989
    2. Separate the love from the taking.Wanting her near is not wrong. Building her voice without the family's yes bypasses both her wishes and theirs.
    3. Give the love somewhere to go.Tell her stories, tend her grave, sit with her family at the anniversary, be a grandchild to her grandchildren.
    4. If the family agrees, keep it to playback of her real voice, for a season, with an end.
    “Bear ye one another's burdens.”Galatians 6:2 (KJV). Many pastors read this as making mourning a congregation's work, not an app's.
    1. Talk before the death.Write down wishes about data and replicas. The take-home card below is a start. Thacker, ERLC
    2. Keep children safe.Cambridge urges age limits; one modeled bot promised a dead mother's reunion with her son. Cambridge
    3. Read the contract.A subscription can bind the heirs.
    4. Extend bereavement ministry past the funeral with intergenerational storytelling and memorial practice. Baptist Standard
    “O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee.”2 Samuel 18:33 (KJV). David grieving a son he was estranged from. The conversation that never happened is one of the oldest griefs there is.
    1. Name what is missing.A sudden death, or a relationship that was broken when it ended, leaves no last conversation. A grief counselor Infera spoke with lost his father at 16, in the middle of years of conflict, and never got that talk. REPORTED interview
    2. See what the bot offers.A griefbot lets you stage the reconciliation you wanted. As the counselor put it, you don't need the bot's consent to make it say what you need to hear.
    3. Know what it cannot give.A bot cannot forgive you, because it was never wronged and cannot consent. Forgiveness it generates is your wish in their voice, the line Dar al-Ifta draws and the Arizona court named. INFERENCE
    4. Keep the words yours.A letter read at the grave, a prayer, or a counselor's empty-chair exercise lets you say everything without inventing their reply. WATCH clinical technique, check with a clinician
    5. Get help with it.The counselor's advice is that everyone should go through therapy at some point, even people who say they don't need it. Unfinished business is exactly what it is for.

    A member dies, and their likes, prayer requests and livestream comments stay on the church's page. Anyone can read the public ones, and almost anyone can copy them.     In the US, privacy dies with you

    Privacy rights generally end at death; families can only claim their own privacy as survivors. Health records are the main exception, protected for 50 years. Summary of post-mortem privacy law

    REPORTED

    France lets you leave instructions

    French law lets a person leave general or specific directives for their data after death, and voids terms of service that override them. Heirs can close accounts or settle the estate when no directive exists. Loi Informatique et Libertés, art. 85

    REPORTED

    A legacy contact cannot read the messages

    On a memorialized Facebook account, a legacy contact can pin a post, change the photo and download what was shared, but cannot log in, read messages or edit old posts. Frazer

    REPORTED

    Public means copyable

    In hiQ v. LinkedIn the Ninth Circuit found serious questions that scraping public profiles breaks federal hacking law; the case settled in 2022 with hiQ found in breach of the site's terms. Public comments by the dead sit in that gray zone. Proskauer

    REPORTED

    Visiting graves softens the heart

    A hadith on visiting graves: “they soften the heart, shed tears in the eyes, and remind of the Hereafter” (al-Bayhaqi 7198, graded sahih by al-Albani). Synagogues keep yahrzeit boards that light a name on the anniversary. WATCH Daily Hadith

    INFERENCE

    What protects a church that does this

    A church's own memorial site is its own religious speech, and churches have wide latitude over their own worship and membership. Comments others post are hosted under platform law, and accounts pass under digital-asset law. None of it was written for a churchyard. Check with counsel. WATCH

    INFERENCE

    Not every tradition has a grave

    Hindu cremation and rites like shraddha don't center on a grave to visit, and Catholic teaching keeps remains in a sacred place, not in keepsakes. In Infera's reading, a digital churchyard should remember the dead, never stand in for the body's resting place.

    ROOM 12 · NOW WE KNOW IN PART

    The bot may know someone you never met.

    A griefbot is a composite of whatever it is fed. Feed it every account a person kept, from the church posts to the second profile, the 2 a.m. searches and the private journal, and it can become someone the family never knew. Then what do you keep: the whole person, or only the good parts?

    “For now we see through a glass, darkly… now I know in part.” 1 Corinthians 13:12 (KJV).

    REPORTED

    Kindness and truth

    Jewish law lets a eulogist praise the dead “a little” beyond their merits, but forbids inventing virtues they did not have. The one who exaggerates “is causing evil to himself and to the deceased” (Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh De'ah 344:1). Jewish Press

    REPORTED

    Mention the good

    A widely cited hadith: “Mention the good about your deceased and refrain from speaking about their evil” (Tirmidhi 1019, Abu Dawud 4864; Tirmidhi calls its chain weak). It counsels restraint in what you say, not inventing what was never true. INFERENCE Hadith Answers

    FACT

    Consent is specific

    Ethicists Spitale and Germani argue a ghost “should draw strictly on user-provided and consented data,” warn it “might reveal things the person never chose to share publicly,” and say the dead should not be turned “into something they were not.” A family reading a diary is different from an AI speaking from it. arXiv 2511.20094, 2025

    FACT

    Users already sand the edges

    The largest study of real users found people actively curate their bots. Memories became idealized, and AI-made “new memories” blended with real ones. Among the 26 users studied, the good-parts bot was already the default. Li and Lan, CHI 2026

    Preparing a family for what is left

    1. Decide before the death.The person, not the survivors, should say what a voice of them may learn from. The take-home card now asks.
    2. Inherit the data before building a voice.Executors get access to accounts; that is not permission to feed them to a bot. US law's default already gives executors who wrote to whom, not what was said. REPORTED
    3. Read with someone beside you.If the family will open the private record, do it slowly, with a pastor, counselor or trusted friend, not alone at 2 a.m.
    4. Expect a stranger in places.Every person had rooms their family never entered. Finding one does not cancel the person you knew.
    5. Leave other people's secrets alone.Private messages hold the living: an ex, a friend, a confessor. A bot should not repeat them.
    6. Mourn the person you knew; entrust the rest.Many traditions hold that only God sees the whole heart. “Man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7, KJV). INFERENCE pastoral framing

    BOTH SIDES · AND THE LIMITS

    What each side can fairly claim.

    The case for a gentle yes

    • Users who choose freely mostly use griefbots as a short bridge and know it is a bot. REPORTED
    • Continuing bonds with the dead can be healthy; ancestor practice in many cultures assumes an ongoing relationship. FACT (as cited by Morris and Brubaker)
    • Dar al-Ifta allows a faithful likeness for remembrance and prayer. FACT
    • Sociologists argue “digital necromancy” extends photos, relics and letters, and fears are overblown. REPORTEDscholars' argument

    The case for caution

    • We found no outcome trials; prolonged grief is a named risk no one has measured in griefbot users. INFERENCEsearch result
    • The dead are held on someone else's servers; bankruptcies and retirements have already caused second deaths. REPORTED
    • A generative bot puts words in the mouth of the dead, the line several traditions draw. INFERENCE
    • The Vatican says AI “can only simulate” relationships and should never be passed off as a person. FACT
    • Limits. Every study here is small or qualitative. No survey breaks griefbot attitudes down by religion or generation, so the spiritual effect on a religious generation is unmeasured. That gap is itself a finding.
    • No formal ruling on griefbots exists from the Southern Baptist Convention, a major rabbinic body, or a Hindu or Shinto institution. Mourning-period lengths are marked WATCH.
    • No case or study covers bots of non-relatives, or estates sold while a bot of the dead remains active. Room 04 is a framing exercise, not a reported case.
