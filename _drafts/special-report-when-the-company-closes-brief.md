## Compression Point

> ***"When data is a cost, estates destroy it. When it is an asset, they sell it. When it is a person, nobody has standing."***

**Short form, for the masthead:** *"The company closed. Your file did not."* (v1's compression point, widened from hospital to company.)

**Reader-arrival framing:** *You have been told what happens to your data when you die (EP 10). This is the other death: the institution holding you goes first. A hospital, an airline, a DNA kit, a chatbot. The bankruptcy court's first question is whether your history is worth more kept, sold or shredded. Nobody in that room is there for you.*

## Structure · Four rooms and a ledger

### Room 1 · The Hospital (formerly *When the Hospital Closes*)

Kept from v1, reframed by Brief 002:

- **Going concern vs. dead estate.** Atlanta Medical Center (Fulton County, 2022) closed with 62 days' notice, but Wellstar survived and kept the records (as published in Brief 002). Crozer's parent, Prospect Medical, is in Chapter 11; the court approved destruction because storage costs money the estate does not have. ChristianaCare's imaging purchase is the exception that survives.
- **Hahnemann, 2019:** records scattered across successor parties; access took months (as published in Brief 002).
- **The clinical aftermarket** (v1's aggregator section, kept): IQVIA, Veeva's Symphony Health, Komodo, Truveta, Datavant. Real-world evidence is the dominant use; AI training is growing.
- **Biospecimens** (v1, kept, without sensationalism): residual samples change hands when institutions divest.

*Season I callback:* Atlanta Medical Center is S1·03's own geography. The Closed Hospital tracked the outcome lag; this room tracks the records lag.

### Room 2 · The Airline

Brief 006's lot, retold for readers who never saw the auction: a deidentified workplace archive, roughly 100 million emails and 17,000 employees, consumer profiles excluded (REPORTED). The union's argument that deidentification cannot hold while "referential integrity" is preserved meets the published science: Sweeney's 87% (ZIP, birth date, sex) and Rocher et al.'s 99.98% from 15 attributes (FACT, peer-reviewed). The worker's door has no handle under §101(41A) (FACT, statute).

WATCH: the September 9 hearing outcome. Score Brief 006's pre-registered branches against the SDNY docket before publication and print which branch held, including if it was the one we did not list.

### Room 3 · The Genome

23andMe as the high-water mark of protection: the FTC chair's letter that a buyer must honor the privacy policy (FACT, Mar 31, 2025), a court-appointed ombudsman, nine-figure stakes, conditions in the sale order (REPORTED). And the one recommendation the court declined: opt-in consent (REPORTED, treat source with caution). The ombudsman's sentence on the dead lives here in full.

### Room 4 · The Companion

Character.AI, live. Disney's $20 million non-exclusive license excludes user chats and data (REPORTED). Those remain in the estate, available to any bidder by November 20 (REPORTED, INFERENCE). Users told they are not creditors. Attorneys general of 39 states and DC filed a notice reserving rights and setting conditions (FACT). The company's privacy policy discloses transfer in a sale, which may let the debtor argue no ombudsman is required (INFERENCE). Export takes three to four weeks (REPORTED), so the export race described in Brief 007 is real.

This is the room where the dead appear inside someone else's data: a user's chat about a late mother is the user's record, not hers.

### The Ledger · "Who was in the room?"

Every room ends at the same table: who appeared in court for the people inside the records. Hospital: the patients were not parties. Airline: a union, partly. Genome: an ombudsman and objecting states (an AG association filing for 28, plus four more). Companion: states, and so far no ombudsman. The reader watches representation thin as the data gets more intimate.

## Interactive Core · *Keep, Sell, or Shred*

The reader is the bankruptcy judge. Four lots arrive, one per room, each with its storage cost, its bid, and the people inside it. Three buttons: **Keep** (who pays?), **Sell** (to whom, under what conditions?), **Shred** (what is lost?). After each choice, the page shows what the real court did, labeled.

The final screen sums the reader's rulings and shows the mirror: *"You protected the customers. Did you protect the dead? The statute never asked you to."*

v1's *Your Body's Paper Trail* tracker survives as Room 1's embedded module, trimmed to the five-year horizon where the evidence is strongest.

## Counter-Read (hosted)

1. **Bankruptcy works.** Creditors deserve recovery; data is an asset like any other; courts already add conditions when privacy is at stake (23andMe proves it).
2. **Research benefit.** Clinical aggregates speed approvals and surveillance.
3. **Deidentification is real protection for most uses.** Imperfect is not useless.
4. **Destruction is a privacy win.** Shredding Crozer's records after notice prevents a sale; some patients would choose that.

Response, carried from v1: each defense holds at its own scale, and the compound effect is invisible to the person inside the records by design.

## Discipline

We did NOT prove any custodian broke a law, that deidentification failed in these specific sales, or how Character.AI's estate will resolve. We DID document four lawful pathways (destroy, sell, transfer with conditions, hold in limbo) and that the statute's protection keys to the living consumer.
