# Interaction Library · how readers take in and keep what we publish

**Status:** standing reference, opened 9 November 2026. Read with Editorial Standard v2 §10 and §11. Add a row every time a page ships a new mechanic; never delete rows.

## Why this exists

Every interaction on the site has to teach a fact or a method; if it would work as well with made-up data, it gets cut. That rule says what an interaction is *for*. This file covers the other half: what *kind* of interaction fits a given job, what we have already used too often, and what we haven't tried yet. The aim is that a returning reader keeps meeting something new, and that what they learn is still there a week later.

**Baseline, 9 Nov 2026:** 83 range sliders across the site, led by The Feed (7), The Chain of Title (6), EP 09 (5) and Field Note 19 (3). The House Already Knew is the first exhibit built with none. Sliders remain allowed where the reader genuinely sets a continuous quantity; they stop being the default.

## Part A · Methods: what makes information stick

Each method is a design principle from learning research. Use it to pick the mechanic, not the other way round.

| Method | What it means for a page | Mechanics that apply it |
|---|---|---|
| **Predict, then reveal** (generation effect; hypercorrection) | Readers remember an answer better when they guessed first, and best when they guessed confidently and were wrong | Over/under slip, Call It, sort the data, guess the line before the chart draws |
| **Retrieval practice** | Being asked to recall something beats reading it again | End-of-room recall card, "which of these did you just see?", motif hunt across rooms |
| **Spacing** | The same idea met again later, in a different form | A fact introduced in room 1 returns as a choice in room 5; cross-episode callbacks |
| **Dual coding** | Words plus a matching picture beat either alone | Shot chart, blueprint plates, the engine diagram |
| **Concrete before abstract** | A person or object first, then the rule | Quarter scoreboard before the legal duty; the $30B building before 3.6% of GDP |
| **Worked example, then fading** | Show one solved case, then let the reader do the next | Capital stack preset, then their own numbers |
| **Desirable difficulty** | Small, fair friction aids memory; confusion does not | A sort where the categories look alike, followed by the explanation of why |
| **Embodied and spatial** | Moving through a space anchors order and scale | Doors, rooms, a court, a map, scroll-as-timeline |
| **Contrast pairs** | Two things side by side reveal what neither shows alone | Two whistles, two doors, Seam pairs |
| **Narrative transport** | A story with stakes carries facts further than a list | Four quarters, the cold open, the end-card cliff |
| **Self-reference** | Facts about "you" are kept longer | Rent ledger, surface dial, "pick your age" |
| **Elaborative interrogation** | Asking *why* deepens learning | Coach's challenge, "throw the flag", Tag It |

## Part D · Picking the mix for a page

1. Name the one thing the reader must keep. Choose the method in Part A that fits it.
2. Use at least three different mechanic families on a page; no single mechanic more than twice.
3. At least one mechanic per new page comes from Part C or is new to the site. When it ships, move it to Part B.
4. Sliders only where the reader sets a continuous quantity that the page then uses.
5. Every result also exists as a sentence (Read the argument), and every mechanic has a keyboard path and a reduced-motion state.
6. After launch, note in the brief what readers did with it if analytics allow (completion, replays, exits), and carry the lesson into this file.
