## Why this is the consequence-half opener

EP 02–04 described the commercial composite machine. EP 05 demonstrated the surveillance substrate. EP 06 described the parallel civic composite. The reader leaves EP 06 knowing **what** the machines are. EP 07 is the first episode of the **consequence** half: it shows the reader they are already paying for the machine they have spent six episodes learning about.

This is the move that converts the season from analytical to material. The cost is named. The cost is local. The cost is on the reader's own utility bill.

EP 07 is built to flip the order with EP 08 deliberately: physical-plant first, then sorting-outcome. The reader has to see the **infrastructure they're funding** before they can fully feel the **outcomes that infrastructure produces** in EP 08.

## Three-act internal structure

### Act 1 · WHAT IT IS (the building)

What is actually inside a modern hyperscale data center, what surrounds it, who owns it. This is the original architecture material from the May-27 draft.

- *Inside the building:* server rows in industrial-density racks, storage arrays, network switches, fiber distribution, UPS battery banks, diesel backup generators sized to run the facility for hours to days, switchgear yards, chillers and CRAH units, increasingly liquid cooling for AI workloads. Fire suppression. Physical security perimeters. Almost no people.
- *Around the building:* substations sized to a small city, cooling towers, water intake, fiber rights-of-way, buffer land, often more buildings on the same campus.
- *Who owns it:* four hyperscalers (Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta) plus a colocation REIT layer (Equinix, Digital Realty, CyrusOne, Iron Mountain). Government-contractor architecture (AWS GovCloud, Azure Government, Google Public Sector) runs the same pattern inside federally-accredited variants — often in the same campuses as commercial workloads.

### Act 2 · WHO PAYS (the new spine)

The episode's load-bearing turn. Data centers do not pay for their own grid upgrades; the transmission and generation costs to serve their load are typically socialized through utility rate cases, often without the data center named in the filing.

- **The rate-payer mechanism.** Public utility commissions approve transmission upgrades and capacity additions. The cost is distributed across the utility's full customer base, including residential. The data center pays its negotiated tariff; the household pays a marginally higher fixed cost forever. The same shape repeats in Northern Virginia, Maricopa County, central Ohio, and the Pacific Northwest.
- **The Vogtle proof-of-concept.** Georgia Power's ratepayers are still paying down a nuclear-plant overrun — Plant Vogtle Units 3 and 4 — whose new load growth is now substantially data-center-driven. The Vogtle story (already documented as S1·07 The Vogtle Bill) is the cleanest case study of the mechanism. The episode hosts it explicitly and links back to the original S1 reporting.
- **The Data Center Load companion (S1·07).** The Season I piece *The Data Center Load* runs in parallel with this episode and shows the residential customer's bill: where the line items go, what percentage is attributable to data-center load growth (current Georgia anchor: ~76% of buildout cost absorbed by residential/small-commercial customers), what the trajectory looks like to 2030. EP 07 references it; it references EP 07. The two pieces are mirrored.
- **What it costs the host community.** Property-tax abatements that reduce local revenue (offered as economic-development incentives), modest operational headcount, contested water rights in regions where water is already politically scarce, transmission-cost socialization, heat and noise emissions, and increasingly substantive local opposition.

### Act 3 · WHAT IT CONNECTS (the convergence)

The physical convergence that the season's spine demands: the same buildings host both the commercial composite that prices you and the civic composite that binds you. The fragmentation that EP 06 named as the civic composite's structural defense exists at the legal-data level. **It does not exist at the physical-plant level.** Commercial AWS and AWS GovCloud frequently share campuses. Commercial Azure and Azure Government share campuses. Both composites — commercial and civic — physically converge in roughly the same handful of facilities owned by roughly the same handful of corporate actors.

The reader leaves Act 3 with the realization: the local ratepayer is funding the substation that powers both machines. The fragmentation that protects the citizen in the legal frame collapses at the physical frame.

## Six-Act Chassis · pinned to the realization arc

### HERO · *Familiarity*
> *You've never seen the building. You think about it every day. Today, you'll see what it costs you.*

### PRESSURE · *Unease*
"The cloud" as a successfully deceptive metaphor. The cloud is land (Loudoun VA, Maricopa AZ, Umatilla OR, Grant WA). The cloud is electricity (≈4.4% of U.S. consumption in 2023; DOE projects 6.7–12% by 2028). The cloud is water. The cloud is fiber. The cloud is generators. The cloud is concrete. The cloud is heat. The cloud is **owned**.

### PIPELINE · *Recognition* (Acts 1 + 2 above)

### OUTCOME · *Implication*
Disparate impact across three populations:
- *Host-county residents* bear local externalities (water, noise, transmission, opposition) in exchange for benefits (tax revenue, construction jobs).
- *Broader utility ratepayers* bear grid-upgrade costs through socialized investments approved by public utility commissions, often without explicit attribution.
- *Communities denied data centers* bear the opportunity cost.

And the wider implication, named directly: **the same buildings host both composites; the fragmentation that protects you legally collapses physically; the bill arrives anyway.**

### COMPETING · *Systems Realization (one half)*
Four defenses hosted fairly: economic development, infrastructure modernization, AI competitiveness as national-economic and national-security priority, and renewables build-out catalyzed by hyperscaler PPAs. Each holds in part. Honest tension: the same buildings driving renewable build-out are also driving fossil-plant retention in regions where renewable capacity cannot yet absorb the load.

### DISCIPLINE · *Systems Realization (other half)*
Three refusals: no conspiracy (decades of procurement and tax-incentive policy, not coordination), no dystopia (data centers do useful work), no mind-control framing (this is an infrastructure episode, not a persuasion episode).

What we did NOT prove:
- We did not prove any specific hyperscaler exceeded its authority in any specific siting decision.
- We did not prove the political-economy relationships are intentionally opaque rather than accidentally complex.
- We did not prove the 2028 energy share projection is the most likely outcome.
- We did not prove the rate-payer subsidy is universally larger than the local-economic benefit.

What we *did* prove: the buildings exist, ownership concentration is real, the energy and water externalities are documented, the rate-payer subsidy mechanism is documented and named-by-jurisdiction, and the physical convergence of commercial and civic composites at the data-center level is verifiable.

### CLOSER
> *"You paid for the machine. The next episode is what it sorts you into — and where you've already seen it happen."*

This is the deliberate foreshadow into EP 08 The Class Engine. The line earns its hook because EP 07 has just established the cost; EP 08 will establish the sorting outcome AND prove via Season I callbacks that the reader has watched it happen to specific families.

## Interactive Core · The Data Center Reveal (extended)

Three-stage participatory mechanic, now four with the cost layer:

**Stage 1 · WHAT'S INSIDE.** Cinematic cross-section. Reader scrolls; layers reveal perimeter → loading docks → mechanical yard → data hall → NOC. Each layer surfaces one quantitative anchor (square footage, power draw, water consumption, rack density, fiber count).

**Stage 2 · WHO OWNS IT.** Geographic map of U.S. data-center concentration, color-coded by owner class. Filter by class. Top markets surface first; secondary markets on demand.

**Stage 3 · WHAT IT COSTS HOST.** A representative host community's fiscal-and-utility picture before and after data-center development. Tax revenue trend, electricity rate trend, water-use trend, transmission cost trend, employment trend — each annotated with documented contribution and documented externality.

**Stage 4 (NEW) · WHAT IT COSTS YOU.** The reader enters their state. The view shifts to a representative residential utility bill for that state and surfaces the estimated annual contribution to grid upgrades supporting data-center load growth. The number is conservative, modeled, and explicitly labeled. The point is not precision; the point is making the cost visible. Closing reveal: *"This is what the line item would look like if it were itemized. It isn't. The cost is still there."*

The Stage 4 reveal is the moment that earns the closer.

## Visual Grammar

**Substation Field** — stylized aerial of a representative data-center campus in the dark editorial palette. Buildings as low-rise rectangular masses. Substations as gridded yards. Cooling towers as cylindrical accents. Fiber rights-of-way as bright connective lines extending offscreen. Motion: a slow breathing pulse from substation through buildings (electrical load), counter-pulse of heat plumes rising from cooling infrastructure (thermodynamic dispatch). Static aerial, kinetic internal flow. The argument is in the flow.

For Act 2 (the cost spine), introduce a second visual: **The Rate-Payer Diagram** — a small repeating glyph showing where a dollar on a residential utility bill goes. Most slices unsurprising. One slice — labeled, animated to pulse — going to "Transmission Upgrades · Data Center Load Growth." The reader sees their own dollar travel into the substation field.
