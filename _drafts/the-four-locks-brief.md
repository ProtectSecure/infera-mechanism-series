# Story brief · The Four Locks (working title)

Opened 11 Nov 2026. Commission: Intake 01 decision 5 (War Room piece on Maryland's wiretap and AI-evidence debate), reframed in Intake 02.

## 1. Reader question
When someone in Maryland secretly records a violent crime against them, what stands between that recording and a courtroom, and what would the 2025 and 2026 bills actually have changed?

## 2. Contribution sentence
Existing coverage (Maryland Matters, WYPR, WMAR, Nov 2025; advocacy posts by MCASA) establishes that Maryland requires all-party consent, that bills to admit victims' recordings were filed, and that the public defender argued consent guards against AI-fabricated audio. Infera contributes: (a) the version record, showing SB 661 changed mid-session from a judge's-discretion test to a lawful-recording rule for three crimes, which closes the conflict the Judiciary flagged on HB 802; (b) the two-session cross-chamber pattern (evidence bills pass the Senate, penalty bills pass the House, neither gets a committee vote in the other chamber); (c) a separation of four questions (lawful, real, admissible, shareable) showing the AI concern is an authentication question that no version changed; (d) a scenario test of what each version does. We investigate through bill texts, amendments, fiscal notes, vote records, written testimony and appellate authority.

## 3. Human situation
Calvert County prosecutor Rebecca Cordero's 20 Feb 2026 written testimony on HB 132: a woman started her phone's recorder before a visit with their daughter; her estranged husband raped her at gunpoint; the prosecutor told her the recording could not be admitted.
- Reporting basis (ours): public written testimony, read directly. No contact with the woman; no attempt to identify her. Outcome of the case unknown; the letter does not say a court ruled.
- Reader entry point: most readers own a phone that can record; most do not know recording can be a felony in Maryland. Do not imply the case is typical; it is one account offered by an advocate for the bill.
- Access decision: a document-led reconstruction, not a personal narrative. Minimal detail; content note at top.

## 4. Primary approach
Reconstruction (of a legislative decision across versions), supported by comparison (versions and two other states). Systems explanation stays secondary.

## 6. Competing explanation
Why no House committee vote: (H1) time; (H2) committee wanted a broader rewrite; (H3) insufficient committee support; (H4) cross-chamber stalemate. Causes may combine. Diagnostic: HB 132/HB 802 heard seven weeks before sine die with no vote weakens H1 as a sole cause; the chair sponsoring HB 802 complicates H3. No committee vote exists, so no tally. Status: undistinguished. Page says so.

## 7. Agency question
- People in the story: the woman chose to record; the law then removed that evidence. Survivors face a choice between documenting and a felony exposure.
- Institutions: House Judiciary and Senate JPR (whether a bill gets a vote); State's Attorneys (charging); the Rules Committee and Supreme Court of Maryland (authentication rules).
- Reader: read the latest version of a bill, use witness sign-up when hearings are set, ask committee members why a bill did not get a vote. No direction is urged.
- Lever map rows feed the impact ledger. "No effective individual remedy has been demonstrated" is not claimed; it is unnecessary here.

## 8. Ending and boundary
Sufficient answer: what each version would have done, what happened to each, and why the AI concern sits with authentication. Out of scope: why the bills failed (needs reporting), the fair-housing tester bills, police body cameras, one-party conversion proposals. Stop rule: no further step could change a published claim except reviewing the briefing video (would upgrade one attribution) and asking DLS about the fiscal-note line; both logged as open checks, neither blocks.
