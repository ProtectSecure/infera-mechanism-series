The Price of No

Saying no to data collection or AI used to be free. Now it often costs a feature, a discount, a license or a special legal status. The people who can afford to refuse are not always the ones who most need to. In a few places, the law has made no cheap again.

01The bargain

No is still allowed. It just costs something now.

On Nov. 3, Google told the country's schools what no will cost them. Early next year, Gemini, Gemini Notebook and Gemini in Classroom will be off by default for K-12 student accounts until an administrator turns them on. A district that keeps them off also loses Google Search for its students, who will see a message saying search is not available. FACT "Search and A.I. are deeply integrated," said Chris Phillips, Google's vice president for education. REPORTED The New York Times.

  1. Aug. 10, 2026

    The Gemini tab in Classroom turns on by default for students of all ages. Switching it off only for students under 18 takes a separate administrator setup. FACT Google Workspace Updates.

  2. July to Oct. 2026

    A youth safety assessment finds Google's AI answers in search missing signs of crisis in tests run as 11- and 15-year-olds; Google calls the queries contrived. Schools can enforce SafeSearch and block the Gemini app, but cannot switch off AI answers in search and keep ordinary results. REPORTED eWeek. Sens. Richard Blumenthal and Josh Hawley write to Google's chief executive about AI in schools. REPORTED

  3. September 2026

    New York City and Los Angeles, the two largest districts, restrict generative AI for younger students on school devices. FACT

  4. Nov. 3, 2026

    Google reverses the default to off, and makes search depend on saying yes. Former FTC chair Lina Khan calls it "using its search monopoly to coerce schools into adopting AI." REPORTED AP.

The federal court order in the search monopoly case, entered Dec. 5, 2025 and now on appeal, bars exclusive deals for Search and Gemini and bars Google from conditioning payments or Play licensing on phone makers and carriers avoiding rivals. Its terms, as reported, address browsers, phone makers and carriers, not schools. REPORTED Whether tying a school's search access to its AI choice falls outside that order is the open question Khan is raising. INFERENCE

Ford's new privacy promise is direct about it: "You absolutely can choose not to share connected vehicle data with Ford," but "some features like BlueCruise, internet connectivity, and vehicle location data on the app will not be usable." FACT That is the modern bargain in one sentence. The feature and the data come as a bundle, and refusing one means losing the other.

The price varies. For a privacy-conscious web user in California, refusing is now one browser setting backed by fines. For a worker who does not want to use an AI tool, the sturdiest protection on offer is a religious accommodation. For a driver ordered into a speed limiter, the alternative is losing the license. 03The faith exemption

The strongest way to refuse AI at work runs through belief

In late August, David Durman, a research administrator at the University of Kentucky and a humanist, asked the university's Office of Equal Opportunity to excuse him from personally using AI. It approved the request on Sept. 25. He still performs all his duties, and the office found no undue burden on his department. FACT The American Humanist Association cites Title VII and, because the university is public, the Constitution. FACT In May, a Unitarian Universalist software developer in North Carolina, Erin Maus, won a similar accommodation from an employer that was not named. FACT

Title VII protects sincerely held religious beliefs, and since the Supreme Court's 2023 Groff v. DeJoy decision, an employer must show an accommodation would bring "substantial increased costs" before it can say no. FACT Employment lawyers expect AI objections to be hard to reject on cost grounds, and warn employers not to judge how sincere a belief is. REPORTED Bloomberg Law. They point to a 2015 case in which a coal miner who refused a biometric hand scanner as the "mark of the beast" won more than half a million dollars. REPORTED

Three limits matter. No employer is known to have denied an AI accommodation, so Groff has not been tested on AI at all. REPORTED HR Dive, Bloomberg Law. Kentucky says accommodations "may be reviewed to ensure they remain reasonable and workable," and lawyers expect undue hardship to get easier to prove as AI spreads through a job. REPORTED And the law protects belief, not preference: a worker with a secular objection, such as worry about quality or privacy, has no comparable shield unless a union contract supplies one. 05Counterexamples

Where saying no got cheap

The lag is not universal. In each of these, someone attached a cost to ignoring a refusal, and the price of saying no fell. INFERENCE

One setting, every site: Global Privacy Control

  1. Aug. 2022

    Sephora, $1.2 million. California's first public privacy-law settlement, built partly on ignoring the browser opt-out signal. FACT

  2. July 1, 2025

    Healthline, $1.55 million. Must honor the signal and test its opt-out tools. FACT

  3. Sept. 9, 2025

    Three states, one sweep. California, Colorado and Connecticut jointly check whether companies honor the signal. FACT

  4. Sept. 30, 2025

    Tractor Supply, $1.35 million. The largest fine in the agency's history, including for failing to honor opt-out signals and for leaving job applicants uninformed. An officer must certify compliance for four years. FACT

  5. Jan. 1, 2027

    AB 566. Every browser must include an opt-out signal setting. Before the law, only Firefox, Brave and DuckDuckGo offered one natively; Chrome and Safari did not. REPORTED

The limit: the signal covers the sale and sharing of personal data under state law. It does not stop a site from collecting data itself or from using it to train AI, and it carries legal weight only where a state recognizes it. INFERENCE

Contracts that make consent real

Video game performers ratified a SAG-AFTRA agreement on July 9, 2025, with 95.04% approval, after a 320-day strike. Studios must disclose every planned use of a performer's voice, likeness and movement and get written consent, and performers can suspend that consent during a strike. FACT In February 2026, New York nurses won AI safeguards at three major hospital systems for the first time; the terms are not public. REPORTED Protection follows the union card. INFERENCE

A right to opt out of the machine

California's privacy agency approved rules on July 24, 2025 giving people a right to opt out of automated decisions in lending, housing, jobs, education and health care, starting Jan. 1, 2027. A business can offer a human appeal instead of the opt-out. REPORTED Colorado went the other way, scaling back its AI Act before it took effect. REPORTED

Telling listeners what they are hearing

Deezer has labeled AI-generated music since 2025, removes AI tracks tied to fraudulent streams, and says more than half its daily uploads, about 90,000 tracks a day at a June 2026 peak, are AI-made. REPORTED Apple made AI tags mandatory for labels and distributors on Aug. 20, but the tags are self-declared, the visible label is not live, and no penalty is published. REPORTED The grief song reported at No. 1 on U.S. iTunes on Nov. 2 carried no AI label on its Apple listing. FACT

06Practical

How to say no today

Turn on the browser signal

  1. Brave and DuckDuckGo send Global Privacy Control by default. FACT
  2. Firefox: turn it on in privacy settings. FACT
  3. It has legal force under California law and similar state laws; elsewhere it is a request. REPORTED

Ask for a human on a health denial

  1. Read the denial notice for the name of the clinical reviewer.
  2. Ask the plan in writing whether an automated tool was used and whether a licensed clinician made the final decision. REPORTED
  3. File the appeal, and contact your state insurance regulator; in Maryland, the Maryland Insurance Administration.
  4. Self-funded employer plans may fall under federal rather than state rules; check your plan documents. INFERENCE

Ask HR for an accommodation

  1. Put the request in writing: the belief, the conflict, and alternatives that still get the work done.
  2. Expect questions about cost, not about whether the belief is sincere. REPORTED
  3. Without a religious basis, look to a union contract or company policy instead. INFERENCE

Opt out of the car, eyes open

  1. Ford: center display or the Ford app; expect BlueCruise and app location to stop. FACT
  2. Turning down an insurer's tracking app means giving up a discount of roughly 5% to 40%; some programs can also raise rates. REPORTED
  3. See what brokers already hold on you: Brief № 013's file guide.

Say no on an iPhone

  1. At iOS 27 setup, tap Not Now on the Siri improvement question. FACT
  2. Already agreed? Settings, Privacy and Security, Analytics and Improvements: turn off Improve Siri and Apple Intelligence and Improve Siri and Dictation. FACT
  3. Keep a sensitive app out of Siri's search: Settings, Apps, the app, Search, then Show Content in Search off; iOS 27.2 adds Exclude Apps. REPORTED
  4. Want the old assistant back: Screen Time, Content and Privacy Restrictions, Siri, Siri Classic. REPORTED
Information, not legal advice. Rights depend on your state, your plan and your employer.
07Both sides

Fair trade, or consent under pressure?

The case for the bargain

  • Some features cannot work without data; a driver-assist system that relies on connectivity cannot run with it switched off. INFERENCE
  • Discounts reward drivers who share, rather than punishing those who do not, insurers argue. INFERENCE
  • Employers need common tools; lawyers expect accommodations to grow harder to sustain as AI becomes central to a job. REPORTED
  • A speed limiter keeps a driver on the road who would otherwise lose a license. REPORTED

The case against

  • A choice between a feature you paid for and your data is not a free choice; the FTC alleged GM enrolled drivers in data sharing without clear consent. FACT
  • Protection follows status: a faith, a union card, a California address. INFERENCE
  • Those with the least room to refuse, such as students in assigned courses, carry the measured losses. REPORTED
  • Where refusal was made cheap by law, it worked: one browser setting now carries fines behind it. FACT
