The Chain of Title

Who holds the paper on Black creative work. Seven exhibits on four legal instruments that moved the gap between making a thing and owning it, from the inventor's oath of 1858 to the voice-clone takedowns of 2026.

Hero: generative chain drawn in code. No photograph, no real place.

AS OF Oct 30, 2026 READ ~45 min whole · 5 to 7 min per exhibit LAYER Public record · court files · statutes · ledgers METHOD SVNIF

A chain of title is the paper trail a buyer checks before paying for a song, a film, or a catalog: every assignment, every renewal, every reversion, back to the person who made the thing. For most of American history, the trail for Black creative work starts with a break in the first link.

This series follows the paper, not the fame. It asks one question of every era: when the work made money, which document decided who got it? The answers are four instruments, each written into law or contract, each still in force in some form today. We name them, date them, and build a working model of each so you can run the numbers yourself.

READING MEMO · Every exhibit stands alone. Every figure carries a label: Fact is on the record, Reported is a credible account we could not independently confirm, Inference is our arithmetic or reading. Dollar conversions to 2025 use the Minneapolis Fed CPI series (Hoover/Rees estimates before 1913, BLS after) and are orders of magnitude, not audits. Legend and ledger are kept apart on purpose. This is an original Infera investigation from the public record; it is not a summary of any book. For the attorney's history of the same terrain, see Lisa E. Davis, Our Minds Were Always Free (Simon & Schuster, 2026), listed in the sources as a further read.
The spine

Four instruments, one gap

Each exhibit is built around the document that did the work.

  1. 1787 to 1865FactThe gate. An inventor had to swear an oath, including to citizenship, which after Dred Scott (1857) a Black applicant could not truthfully do. An enslaved person could not swear at all, and the enslaver could not swear to an invention he did not make. The work existed; the title could not.3, 4
  2. 1909 to 1977FactThe renewal term. 28 years, then 28 more, and the second term went back to the living author. A song sold for $50 in 1912 came home in 1940, if its author was alive to claim it.2
  3. 1945 to todayFactThe recoupable advance. Royalties are credited, then charged against costs the label controls. A hit can leave the artist owing money for decades.39, 40
  4. 1978 to todayFactThe termination window. 17 U.S.C. §203: thirty-five years after a grant, the author can take it back, with two to ten years' notice, unless a bankruptcy or a work-for-hire clause took the right first.62, 65

The Oath

Link status · broken at the source

In the spring of 1857 an enslaved blacksmith named Ned, on a plantation in Pike County, Mississippi, built a double cotton scraper that did two passes of work in one. His enslaver, Oscar Stuart, applied for the patent in November. The Patent Office sent it back. The problem was not the machine.4

The 1836 Patent Act required the applicant to be the "original and first inventor" and to swear an oath to it. Stuart had not invented anything. Ned, in the Commissioner's view, was not competent to swear. Stuart petitioned Congress twice and backed a bill to let enslavers patent what the people they held had made. It failed. The Confederacy wrote that provision into its own patent law in May 1861, section 50.4

Then came the ruling that closed the gate for everyone. On June 10, 1858, Attorney General Jeremiah Black answered the Interior Department's question about a different enslaved inventor's machine: it "cannot be patented," and a patent issued to the master "would not protect him in the courts." Black leaned on Dred Scott, decided the year before. Since every applicant swore to citizenship, the opinion shut out free Black inventors too, people like Thomas Jennings, who had patented a dry-cleaning process in 1821.3, 4, 5

Why this is the first linkA chain of title begins with an author. The 1858 opinion did not take a title away; it declared that none could come into existence. Every later instrument in this series operates on a title that exists. This one operates before.

The custody chain

Blind Tom, in four guardianships

A composer who could not hold his own copyright, managed by a family that held him.

  1. c. 1850FactThomas Wiggins, born enslaved in Harris County, Georgia, is sold as an infant with his parents to James N. Bethune of Columbus.7
  2. c. 1857 to 58ReportedBethune hires the boy out to promoter Perry Oliver on a three-year, $15,000 contract. Oliver is said to clear about $50,000. Tom and his parents receive nothing.8, 9
  3. 1860 to 61FactA White House performance for President Buchanan. "The Battle of Manassas," composed at 12. Wartime concert proceeds go to Confederate soldier relief.7
  4. 1865ReportedEmancipation. Bethune has Tom's freed parents sign a five-year indenture: Tom to receive $20 a month, his parents $500 a year; the Bethunes keep the rest, by one account about 90 percent. A Black promoter, Tabbs Gross, sues for guardianship in Cincinnati and loses.8, 9
  5. 1870ReportedThe indenture expires. Bethune petitions to have Tom, now 21, declared insane, and himself appointed guardian. Granted.8
  6. 1879ReportedConcerts and sheet music net "close to $20,000 a year." Tom's compositions are published under invented names: W. F. Raymond, J. C. Beckel, François Sexalise.8, 10
  7. 1887FactAfter the death of the son who managed him, the son's widow, Eliza Bethune, wins custody, using Tom's mother Charity as the nominal petitioner. Charity gets no share and returns to Georgia. Eliza bills Tom as "the last slave set free by order of the Supreme Court." No such order exists.10, 9
  8. 1908FactTom dies in Hoboken, New Jersey, in Eliza's home. Lifetime compensation, by the fullest account: room, board, and a Steinway.7, 9

Same instrument, different holder

The songs that built Jubilee Hall

In 1867 three white editors published Slave Songs of the United States, 136 spirituals transcribed from freedpeople on St. Helena Island. No singer is credited. No singer was paid, so far as any record shows. Under the copyright law of the day, the compilation belonged to the editors and their publisher.13 InferenceThe people who made the songs held nothing; the people who wrote them down held the book.

Four years later, nine students from Fisk University left Nashville with the school's last $40 and sang those songs to white audiences in the North. Their first tour raised the $20,000 the school had been promised. The 1873 tour of Britain, including a performance for Queen Victoria, came home with about $50,000. Fisk credits the singers with raising more than $160,000 for Jubilee Hall, dedicated January 1, 1876. In today's money that is roughly $4.8 million.11, 12

They were paid professionals by 1873; no wage figures survive in the published record. The institution owned the revenue. The singers, who endured typhoid, tuberculosis, and hotels that would not seat them, held salaries at best and no authorship interest in what they sang.11 InferenceThis is the gate from the other side: a Black institution could hold title when Black individuals could not, and the music's first owners were a college and a missionary association.

One Cent

Link status · held, at a price

On August 10, 1899, in Sedalia, Missouri, a publisher named John Stark signed a typed contract with Scott Joplin for "Maple Leaf Rag." The terms, witnessed by a local lawyer, were a one-cent royalty on every copy sold at a minimum price of twenty-five cents. The original is in the Morgan Library.16

That is the first link in this series that holds. Joplin kept a royalty. It was small, the first run of 400 copies took a year to sell, and by 1909 the historian Edward Berlin estimates the rag brought him about $600 a year. The stories of 75,000 copies in six months are disproved. But the paper named him, and the paper paid.17

Thirteen years later in Memphis, W. C. Handy sold "The Memphis Blues," plates and copyright, for $50, or $100 by another account, to a buyer who added lyrics and republished it. The Victor Military Band's 1914 recording is in the National Recording Registry. Handy got nothing from any of it, for twenty-eight years.19, 20

Twenty-eight is the number that matters. The Copyright Act of 1909 gave a work 28 years, then a second 28-year term that reverted to the living author. In 1940 "The Memphis Blues" came home. Handy had by then founded his own publishing house with Harry Pace and kept "St. Louis Blues" from the start; at his death in 1958 that song alone was earning him about $25,000 a year.2, 20, 21

"One-cent royalty for each copy sold."Contract, John Stark & Son and Scott Joplin, Aug. 10, 1899 · Morgan Library & Museum · T1

The first Black-owned label

Black Swan, 1921 to 1923

Harry Pace left the Handy partnership in early 1921 and founded Pace Phonograph Corporation in Harlem that March. The board included James Weldon Johnson; the NAACP's magazine invested. Ethel Waters, then earning $35 a week plus tips, asked for $100 to cut "Down Home Blues" and got it. Pace claimed half a million copies in six months. Specialists put it nearer 100,000 to 120,000. Either way the company's first-year sales passed $100,000, and at peak it was pressing 7,000 records a day in a plant built for 6,000.22, 23, 24

Bankruptcy came in December 1923; Paramount bought the label and shut it the next spring. Pace blamed radio. He went on to found an insurance company, earn a law degree, and die in 1943, his own descendants unaware of his ancestry until 2007.23, 24

FactSound recordings had no federal copyright at all until February 15, 1972. Every "race record" of the 1920s was unprotected as a recording. Only the song underneath could be owned, renewed, or reclaimed.2 InferenceThat is why the flat-fee singers of this era, Mamie Smith, Bessie Smith, Ethel Waters, had nothing to renew in 1948 or 1951, and why the rare Black publishers, Pace, Handy, Perry Bradford, held the only asset the 28-year clock could ever return.

Bradford learned the clock's other edge. He sold "Wicked Blues" to one publisher, then copyrighted it as "Crazy Blues" and gave it to Mamie Smith. A New York court in 1921 ordered an accounting of profits. He paid damages, split with Smith, and in the 1940s worked as a mailman.26, 25

Unrecouped

Link status · held on paper, broken in the accounting

Ruth Brown's records built Atlantic. Her contract paid her a royalty of about five percent. When the lawyer Howell Begle took her case in 1983, the label's position was that she owed it money, and that between 1960 and 1980 she had earned $754 in royalties. Atlantic could not document the debt.39, 40

This is the third instrument, and it is the one still in every contract. A royalty is credited, then charged against the advance, the studio, the packaging, the promotion, a reserve against returns. The account runs negative. It can run negative through a career. The artist is not lied to; the artist is told the truth in a column that never reaches zero.

Begle took on thirty more Atlantic artists. Brown testified before a congressional panel. In 1988 Atlantic waived its R&B artists' unrecouped balances, recalculated royalties back to 1970, and put $1.5 million toward founding the Rhythm & Blues Foundation. Brown's first check in twenty-eight years was about $20,000 by one account, $30,000 by another. Most other majors followed.38, 39, 40

The Master's Master

Link status · held, reclaimed, re-sold

In November 1959 Ray Charles signed with ABC-Paramount for one of the highest royalty rates of its day, a $50,000 annual advance, and, the Country Music Hall of Fame records, ownership of his own master recordings. His own site calls it a first for a major recording artist.53

The masters and the publishing are two different deeds. A recording can be owned by the artist while the song underneath belongs to someone else, and the reverse. The fights in this exhibit are about which deed, held by whom, and for how long.

Sam Cooke built both: Kags Music for his songs, SAR Records for other artists, and in 1963 a company called Tracey to own his recordings with RCA funding the sessions. He died without a will in December 1964. Control of Tracey, and in time much of his publishing, passed to the holding company of his manager, Allen Klein.52 InferenceOwnership on paper did not survive the absence of one more piece of paper.

Stevie Wonder's lawyer disaffirmed his minority contracts on May 14, 1971, the day after he turned 21, and renegotiated: a 14 percent royalty, an advance above $900,000, a new publishing deal with Motown's Jobete. In August 1975 came the $13 million guarantee over seven years, which TIME noted was worth as much as Elton John's and Neil Diamond's deals combined.54, 55

Then Jobete itself was sold. In 1997 EMI paid Berry Gordy $132 million for half of a catalog of more than 15,000 songs. In 2003, $110 million for 30 percent more. In 2004, about $80 million for the last fifth. About $322 million in all, roughly $590 million in today's money, for the songbook that Holland-Dozier-Holland had sued over in 1968 with a $22 million counterclaim demanding a receiver over those very copyrights.50, 51, 47

The Screen Deed

Link status · broken for the cast, held for the producer

Fifteen days after the premiere of Gone with the Wind, Hattie McDaniel signed a contract with Selznick International: $500 a week, twenty weeks guaranteed, options rising to $1,500 a week by 1946, exclusivity across film, radio, television, and personal appearances. She would win the Academy Award that spring. The contract was voided when the studio dissolved the next year.71

In television the pattern was set by the first Black-cast network sitcom. Freeman Gosden and Charles Correll, the two white creators of Amos 'n' Andy, sold all rights to CBS in 1948 for a reported $2.5 million. The series ran 1951 to 1953, drew an NAACP campaign calling it "a gross libel," lost its sponsor, and then ran in syndication for thirteen more years, until 1966. The Black cast had no participation in any of it.69, 70

The alternative had existed since 1918, when Oscar Micheaux sold stock to farmers around Sioux City to finance The Homesteader and carried the prints by hand to some 700 theaters. He made more than forty films, bought Charles Chesnutt's novel for $500 in installments and bounced a check on it, and filed for bankruptcy in 1948. There was no studio equity to lose, and no retained library to pass on.67, 68

Thou Shalt Not Steal

Link status · broken by a ruling, priced by a split

Judge Kevin Duffy opened his December 1991 opinion with three words from Exodus. Biz Markie's "Alone Again" had used a piece of Gilbert O'Sullivan's recording without permission. The judge enjoined the record and referred the matter to the U.S. Attorney for possible criminal prosecution.88

Hank Shocklee of Public Enemy's Bomb Squad dates the change to the year before: the law "didn't start catching up with us until Fear of a Black Planet," after which labels began "policing the releases before they got out." Chuck D: a hundred fragments in a song meant a hundred people to answer to, and "it is too expensive to defend against a claim." By 1991 the group had moved to live instruments. Dr. Dre's workaround, Chuck D said, was to replay the part rather than sample it, so only the song's owner, not the recording's, had to be paid.91

In 2005 the Sixth Circuit removed the last defense for recordings: "Get a license or do not sample," over a two-second Funkadelic guitar chord in an N.W.A. track, in a case brought by Bridgeport Music, the company from Exhibit 4. In 2016 the Ninth Circuit disagreed over a quarter-second horn hit in Madonna's "Vogue," creating a split the Supreme Court has not resolved. Where you are sued still decides what a sample costs.90

De La Soul's first six albums, beginning with 3 Feet High and Rising in 1989, reached streaming on March 3, 2023, thirty-four years later, after a clearance tangle and a 2019 offer from their label that would have paid the group 10 percent. Trugoy the Dove died in February 2023, weeks before the catalog went live.92

The Voice Deed

Link status · open · being written now

On April 17, 2023, a track called "Heart on My Sleeve" was pulled from every major service. It had more than 15 million views on TikTok and 625,000 plays on Spotify. The voices were Drake's and The Weeknd's; neither had sung a note. Universal's claim rested on the recorded voice and the affiliation, not the song, which appears to have been original.112

The gate of 1858 asked who could be an author. The question of 2026 is whether a voice is a title at all, and if so, who holds it: the singer, the label with the exclusive contract, the estate, or the company that trained on all three.

The first answer came from Tennessee. The ELVIS Act, signed March 21, 2024, added "voice," actual or simulated, to the state's publicity statute, created liability for distributing tools whose primary purpose is cloning an identifiable person, and let recording artists sue even while under exclusive label contracts.114 The second came from the Copyright Office: a recommendation for a federal digital-replica law (July 2024), a finding that prompts alone do not make an author (January 2025), and a pre-publication report on training (May 9, 2025) that commercial training to produce competing work "goes beyond established fair use boundaries." The Register of Copyrights was dismissed the next day.115, 116

The third is in Congress. The NO FAKES Act was reintroduced May 20, 2026 as a federal, licensable property right in voice and visual likeness, with notice-and-takedown, a 14-day counter-notice window, and a $25,000 floor for knowing misrepresentation. The Senate Judiciary Committee advanced it unanimously in June; the House had not scheduled a markup as of mid-June.118, 119 WatchWhether the House moves before the 119th Congress ends.

The chain, read whole

Four instruments, one direction

Read in order, the exhibits describe a gap that narrows and never closes. The oath barred the first link outright. The renewal term returned a song after twenty-eight years to whoever had survived to claim it. The recoupable advance let the title stay with the artist while the money stayed with the label. The termination window gave the title back after thirty-five years, unless a bankruptcy or three words in a contract took it first. The voice is the newest deed, and the rulebook for it is being written in three legislatures and two courts at once.

InferenceEach instrument was neutral on its face and specific in its effect, because each one priced a thing the holder already had: citizenship, longevity, capital, counsel. Those were not evenly held. The chain of title is a record of who had them when.

What the record supports

  • The 1858 opinion, the one-cent contract, the $125-per-side ledger, the §203 statute, and the 2026 Eleventh Circuit opinion are primary documents (T1).
  • The 28-year reversion, the 1988 Atlantic reform, and the 2014 Prince reversion are documented reclamations, each tied to a dated instrument.
  • Ownership outcomes in television and film track who held the program copyright, not who was on screen.

What it does not

  • Blind Tom's "$100,000 a year," Mamie Smith's "million copies," and Little Richard's exact 1955 terms are legend-grade; we show them hatched or labeled Reported.
  • No public study measures the racial split of catalog-sale dollars or unmatched royalties. Those inferences are flagged.
  • Contract mechanics in the Statement exhibit are reconstructed, not Ruth Brown's actual accounting.
