The Ventriloquist

The dead don't stop talking. Someone else writes their lines.

In the last three years, AI voices of dead people have endorsed candidates, called Congress, preached from pulpits and talked about their own killings. In every political case we found, a living person chose the words: a party, a campaign, a family, or an author no one has named. A griefbot is a ventriloquist's act with a loved one's voice. This note follows who holds the strings eleven days before the midterms, and finds the rules mostly look the other way.

Which rules reach the dead?

Most election rules are written around candidates, and most platform rules only require a label. The laws that do require a dead person's estate to consent are commercial: they protect a voice used to sell a game, not one used to sell a candidate.

A dead actor's voice needs the estate's consent to sell a video game. A dead leader's voice needs no one's consent to sell a candidate. INFERENCE

Whether each state's “candidate” definition leaves out a dead person who is not on the ballot has not been checked statute by statute. Treat the rule gap as a reading of the trackers, not a legal finding.

Both sidesStated at their strongest

The case for letting the dead speak

  • Families are a person's natural stewards. A parent letting a murdered child “speak” is the parent's speech, labeled as such. REPORTED
  • The Shotline families consented and disclosed; an ethicist called it defensible on that basis.
  • Estates already police abuse: the Tupac track came down within days.
  • A researcher who began skeptical said careful “generative ghosts” “could be a really positive thing.”

The case against

  • Consent from the living is not consent from the dead. Once the voice is a product, the steward can speak on topics the person never addressed. INFERENCE
  • Party-made resurrections had no reported family consent: Suharto, Jayalalithaa, King.
  • The most-heard pulpit case still has no known author.
  • Grieving listeners forgive invented facts, and chatbots that persuade more also make more inaccurate claims.

The same test applies to the right-coded Kirk clip and the left-coded Oliver avatar: who wrote it, did the family agree, and is it labeled? On those three questions, the Oliver avatar passes and the Kirk clip fails on authorship. Neither side has a monopoly on the dead. INFERENCE
