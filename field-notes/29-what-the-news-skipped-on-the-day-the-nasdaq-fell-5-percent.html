What the news skipped on the day the Nasdaq fell 5 percent

Tech stocks had one of their worst days in years after a report that regulators are looking at Nvidia's books, and the President spent more than an hour on Xi and Putin. In the same hours, Philadelphia's child welfare chief said her department is not allowed to open the state file that had flagged the foster mother now charged with killing a 10-year-old. That was one of four quiet stories about who gets to read the record, and when.

The file Philadelphia could not open

Malayshia Allford was 10 years old and in foster care. Her foster mother, a former Philadelphia schoolteacher, is charged with her murder. REPORTED

On Friday the whole City Council questioned the head of the city's Department of Human Services, Kimberly Ali. Her most important answer was about a database. Pennsylvania keeps a list called the Resource Family Registry: a record of people who have applied to foster, including abuse findings and appeals. By state law, only the state and the private agencies that approve foster homes can look at it. The city, which pays for and supervises the children's care, cannot. Ali said getting that access is her "number one" priority. REPORTED

The Philadelphia Inquirer reports that a 2022 state review found this same foster mother had abused a 4-year-old foster child and recommended she never foster again, and that the finding was later erased on appeal. REPORTED Council member Cindy Bass: "This death was preventable." She filed a bill Thursday to stop handing children's case management to outside agencies. Mayor Parker ordered a special committee.

0of 6 national outlets led with it

Why this reaches you

If you live in Pennsylvania, the fix is not in City Hall. It is a change to state law in Harrisburg, which means your state representative and senator decide whether the people responsible for a child can read that child's caregiver's file. Anywhere else, the same question applies: ask who in your county is allowed to see the foster-parent record before a placement.

A 150-day clock on your right to object

Late Thursday night the Senate passed a big rewrite of how the federal government approves energy projects, power lines and the data centers that run AI. It is called the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act. The vote was 62 to 33: 31 Democrats voted no, joined by Republicans Josh Hawley and Mike Rounds. REPORTED

The headline change for ordinary people: if you want to challenge a federal permit in court, for a pipeline, a transmission line or a plant near your home, the time you have to file shrinks "from years to 150 days." Courts would also have less power to stop construction while a case goes on. The bill gives the federal energy regulator new power over power lines that cross state borders and narrows the power of states and Tribes to block projects over water pollution. REPORTED

Friday was its first full news day, and it landed under a 5 percent Nasdaq drop. The President called it "a big deal" in passing. The House is expected to vote after Thanksgiving.

150days to sue, down from years

Why this reaches you

Most people learn about a project when the bulldozers arrive. Under this bill, by then the window to challenge it in court may already be shut. If a line or plant is proposed near you, the permit record is the thing to read early. Your House member votes on this after Thanksgiving.

"We do not have that luxury"

Fifteen minutes after the President's news conference began, Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook spoke in Santiago. The Fed sets the base interest rate that every loan in the country is built on. Cook said that in a different economy the Fed "could afford to be patient," but "we do not have that luxury." She said she is "skeptical" that the one quarter-point increase in September will be enough to bring prices down, and that if the Fed falls behind, "additional and larger rate increases may be required later." REPORTED

That makes two Fed governors in 24 hours, after Michael Barr on Thursday, saying more increases may be needed. Inflation by the Fed's preferred measure was 3.8 percent through October. REPORTED One small counterweight came the same morning: households' expectations for inflation over the next year fell to 3.7 percent from 4.3 percent in mid-November, in the University of Michigan survey. REPORTED · single source

3.8%inflation, the number Cook is answering

Why this reaches you

A December rate increase raises the cost of a credit card balance, a car loan or a home equity line within weeks. If you are deciding between locking a rate now or waiting, two governors just told you which way they lean.

The blockade now reaches ships that never touch Iran

The US Navy has been blockading Iran's oil exports. This week the military's Central Command said ships that take Iranian oil from another ship at sea, without ever entering an Iranian port, "may be stopped, boarded, diverted, captured." That widens the blockade to the whole ocean. REPORTED

Friday's reporting added the paper trail. A memo from the federal maritime agency, dated October 12 and released only through a public-records request, warned of "war-risk premium spikes" and crews refusing to sail. OPEC puts the insurance cost of shipping a barrel of Gulf crude to East Asia at $12.29, about 200 times January's level. The UN shipping agency counts about 130 attacks on vessels in this war and at least 25 sailors killed. REPORTED

$12.29insurance per barrel, about 200 times January

Why this reaches you

Insurance on a tanker is part of the price of the fuel it carries. That cost travels into gasoline, heating oil and airfares, and it is one of the reasons the Fed governor in Exhibit III says it cannot wait.

The story that took the oxygen: an unnamed problem at Nvidia

Reuters reported Friday afternoon, citing two people briefed, that staff at the Securities and Exchange Commission are examining "potential accounting issues" at Nvidia. Reuters could not say which issues, or when the inquiry began. The SEC declined to comment, and Nvidia did not immediately respond. REPORTED

Nvidia fell about 14 percent, about $431 billion in value. The Nasdaq fell 5.28 percent, the S&P 500 3.70 percent, and the fear gauge called the VIX closed near 36. Anthropic, in its second day of trading, fell about 8.5 percent. REPORTED · single-source closes

This was real news. The oxygen point is what it was used to explain. The S&P fell about 8.6 percent this week, and a report with no named issue cannot carry that alone. The record that explains more of the week was in Exhibits III and IV: inflation near 4 percent, a war that is raising the price of moving oil, and a Fed that says it is out of patience.

−5.28%Nasdaq, Friday

Why this reaches you

If you hold an index fund, a large share of it sits in a handful of AI companies. A drop like this is the cost of that concentration. The question to ask is not what Nvidia hid, which no one has said, but what interest rates do to companies valued on profits years away.

The podium: claims without documents

The President's first news conference after meeting Xi and Putin in Shenzhen ran over an hour. He said China "agreed for the first time to condemn the abuses and overreach" of the International Criminal Court, that Thailand agreed to "eliminate all tariffs" on American goods, and that there were "$17 billion" in commercial deals across Southeast Asia. He said Putin told him the Ukraine invasion "would have never happened" if Trump were president. On Iran he wants "a real deal." He called the Fed "very political." REPORTED

No text of the Shenzhen declaration, and no trade agreement text or fact sheet, was released that we could find. The coverage ran on the Putin quotes.

Why this reaches you

A tariff deal changes prices only when it is written down. Until a text is published, treat the numbers as an announcement. When one appears, the tariff lines on farm goods and electronics are the parts that touch your bills.

Interconnection · how the stories are one system

The same machine ran under the crash and the quiet stories

The cameras followed the price. Every quiet story on Friday was about a record: who is allowed to read it, and whether they get to read it before it is too late to act.

Read the top row left to right. The war raises the price of moving oil; that price shows up in inflation; inflation is what Cook is answering; higher rates make the AI trade fragile, and the Nvidia report was the match. The AI and power buildout is also what the permitting bill is for. The bottom row is a pattern, not a chain: Philadelphia cannot read the state's foster-parent file, shippers saw a federal warning only months later through a records request, and neighbors of a new project will get 150 days to read its permit and object. Even the loud story fits: investors sold $431 billion of Nvidia on an inquiry whose subject no one outside has seen.

Resolved quietly · logged for the record

North Carolina's Senate will not tie. The recount confirmed Republican Danny Britt by 225 votes, 28,054 to 27,829. Republicans keep a 26 to 24 majority. Democrat Catina Hamm had not conceded Friday afternoon. REPORTED

The federal civil service rule was not a story. We flagged an Office of Personnel Management notice as worth reading. It turned out to confirm an August rule that removes outdated wording; the agency says it changes no rights or protections. FACT
