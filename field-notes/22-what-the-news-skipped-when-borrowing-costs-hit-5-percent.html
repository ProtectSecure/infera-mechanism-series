Field Note № 22 · Evening Close · Tuesday, 10 November 2026

What the news skipped when borrowing costs hit 5 percent

On Tuesday a hot inflation report pushed the interest rate that anchors American mortgages to 5 percent, and an hour after lunch the government borrowed $42 billion at that price. The cameras were pointed at an impeachment vote that was always going to be shelved. Behind the lens: the bond sale, a 5 p.m. ballot deadline in Mississippi, and a strait that is still closed.

IThe price of borrowing touched 5 percent

At 8:30 a.m. the Bureau of Labor Statistics said prices rose 0.4 percent in October, more than the 0.3 percent forecasters expected. The "core" measure, which leaves out food and energy because they swing so much, rose 0.3 percent against a forecast of 0.2. FACT Lettuce was up 7.3 percent in a month, beef 0.9, car insurance 0.7. The government could not say how much prices rose over the full year, because it never collected October 2025 prices during last year's shutdown. FACT

Traders read the number one way: the Federal Reserve will likely raise interest rates at its December 8 to 9 meeting. Fed Governor Christopher Waller had said that a 0.3 percent core reading would justify thinking about a half point increase. Core came in at exactly 0.3. REPORTED

The 10 year Treasury yield, the interest rate the government pays to borrow for ten years, jumped about 0.14 points and closed at 5.00 percent. REPORTED At 1 p.m. the Treasury sold $42 billion of brand new 10 year notes. They cleared at 5.020 percent. Buyers bid 2.46 dollars for every dollar on offer, below the recent average. The share taken by "indirect" bidders, the category that includes foreign central banks and overseas investors, fell to 61 percent from 80 percent at last month's sale. One source says this is the first 10 year note with a 5 percent coupon since 2006. REPORTED

Why this reaches you

Lenders set 30 year mortgage rates by watching the 10 year yield, so when it climbs, the monthly payment on a house you have not bought yet climbs with it. The same rate feeds car loans and business borrowing. And the bond sale is the government's own loan: when fewer overseas buyers show up, taxpayers pay a little more interest on every dollar of new debt. You did not see this on the evening news. You will see it in the next rate quote you get.

IIFifteen thousand ballots and a 5 p.m. deadline

In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom led Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by 2,574 votes on Monday, a gap of 0.28 percent, and the AP had not called the race. REPORTED The Colom campaign says about 15,000 people cast "affidavit" ballots, the kind you fill out when the poll book says you are not on the list. Many of those voters had been marked inactive. To have that ballot counted, a voter had to show acceptable ID to the county by 5 p.m. local time on Tuesday. Mississippi has no recount law, counties must certify by Friday, November 13, and lawyers for Hyde-Smith have asked officials to set the affidavit ballots aside and preserve them. REPORTED

In Alaska, Mary Peltola led Senator Dan Sullivan by 2,517 first-choice votes as of Monday, close to the 50 percent mark that would skip a ranked-choice runoff. Tens of thousands of held-back absentee and early ballots were due to be counted on Tuesday. REPORTED We could not retrieve Tuesday's numbers for either state before this note closed. WATCH

The same day, the president posted asking whether the midterm was honest. REPORTED

Why this reaches you

If you have ever been told at the polls that you are not on the list, this is your story. Whether a provisional ballot counts often comes down to a short window and a trip to the county office with an ID. Tuesday showed how many people that window can touch in one state: more than five times the margin between the candidates.

IIIThe strait is still shut, and oil fell anyway

Iran's negotiator said there are no talks with the United States right now, and Iran denied a reported deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz in exchange for $8 billion in frozen money. Secretary of State Rubio said there is no need for major new combat. REPORTED Oil traders heard calm: Brent crude settled at $91.49, down 1.2 percent and the lowest since August 31. REPORTED

The water told a different story. Three ships crossed the strait on Sunday. Before the war the daily average was 85. REPORTED The UAE's national oil company said one of its vessels was damaged in an attack in UAE waters on Tuesday, a single-source report. Iran's Revolutionary Guard claimed more tanker strikes that no shipping authority has confirmed. REPORTED

Why this reaches you

Gas, heating fuel and the diesel that trucks your groceries are priced partly on what traders expect, and partly on what actually moves through the water. Right now those two are far apart. When they close the gap, the price at the pump and the energy line in next month's inflation report will tell you which one was right.

IVThe story that took the oxygen

Representative Thomas Massie, a Republican, forced a House vote on impeaching Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth amid Republican criticism of how the Iran war has been run. The House voted 211 to 204 to send the measure to committee, which shelves it. Massie was the only Republican who broke with his party. REPORTED Every major network ran a live blog. Our board had scored the vote at 40 out of 100, which is too low for how much attention it drew, the second day in a row we missed it.

The same afternoon House Republicans chose Jim Jordan over Steve Scalise as their leader for the next Congress, 106 to 73 according to people familiar with the count, and Nvidia shares fell 5.5 percent. REPORTED

Why this reaches you

The vote decided that this Congress will not hold a formal debate on how the war is being run. That matters, but the result was predictable from the seat count, and the spectacle filled the hours when the bond sale and the ballot deadline were happening. Attention is the one thing you cannot spend twice.

How the pieces connect

The news presented four separate stories. Here is the single chain that runs through them.

The war the House voted not to examine is the same war in your grocery bill and your mortgage rate. One machine produced both the noise and the number.
