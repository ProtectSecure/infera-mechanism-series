    Field Note № 19 · Money and Machines · Sunday, 8 November 2026

    The Capital Call.

    One of the biggest AI data centers in America was built with about 10 percent down. Here is how the money behind the AI boom works, in plain English.

    Every AI answer you get runs on four things: electricity, computer chips, a building to hold them, and a pile of money to pay for all three. This note walks through each one like a trade school course. By the end you will know why a few lines in a company's paperwork matter to your family's electric bill, your parents' retirement account and maybe your first job.

    On Monday, 26 October 2026, three of the biggest names in tech went looking for money on the same day.

    Microsoft offered voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 workers, about a quarter of its workforce, as its AI spending climbs. A spokeswoman said only a fraction are expected to take them. REPORTED

It also says: "We anticipate making significant and increased investments for the foreseeable future." REPORTEDCBS News; Washington Post · see the conflict note above

    Why so much money? Because the plan is enormous. An economist at Columbia University, Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh, added it up for the Brookings Institution. He estimates the AI buildout in the United States will cost about $10.3 trillion from 2025 to 2032. That works out to about 3.6 percent of everything the country produces in a year, every year, for eight years. FACThis published estimate

    ComputingExhibit 3 · The chipsProvenance: Brookings paper; Gartner forecast, as reported

    The expensive part gets old fast.

    Inside the building are racks of , the chips that do AI's math. They are the most expensive thing in the building, and they age like phones, not like buildings. Newer, faster chips come out every year or two. Loans used to buy them run about four to six years, according to Van Nieuwerburgh, far shorter than the buildings they sit in. FACThis description of the financing

    A whole new kind of company has grown up just to buy these chips and rent out the computing power. They are called . Many of them borrow to buy chips today and hope the rent checks keep coming tomorrow. The research firm Gartner predicts that more than 80 percent of them will stop operating on their own by 2030, either shutting down or being bought. REPORTEDGartner forecast via CRN and Network World, Oct 2026

    If you are thinking about a career in tech, this is the part of the industry hiring fastest and also the part most likely to shake out. Both things can be true at once. INFERENCE

    ArchitectureExhibit 4 · The buildingProvenance: Meta and Blue Owl deal announcements, 21 Oct 2025; Brookings paper

    Who owns the building, and whose books it is on.

    You might assume Meta simply bought Hyperion. It did not. Here is how the deal was set up, step by step:

    1. A new company was created just for this building. Investment funds run by a firm called Blue Owl own most of it. Meta kept about 20 percent. REPORTED
    2. That new company borrowed most of the money by selling to big investors such as PIMCO. REPORTEDadvisers' announcement
    3. Meta then rents the campus back. Its first runs four years, with the option to keep extending. REPORTED
    4. Meta also signed a : a promise to cover some of the loss if the campus is worth less than expected when Meta's lease ends. REPORTEDadvisers' announcement

    Van Nieuwerburgh's paper puts the borrowed share at about $27 billion against a building worth about $30 billion. That is roughly 90 percent borrowed, 10 percent down, and the debt sits on the new company's books, not Meta's. FACThis figures

    Why would it stay off Meta's ? Accounting rules generally require a company to report a future cost only if it is likely to happen. Under the standard he describes, a commitment judged less than about 70 percent likely does not have to appear there. FACThis description of the rule So the guarantee can be real and still not show up as debt. Off the balance sheet is not the same as off the hook.

    BankingExhibit 5 · The stackProvenance: Brookings paper; Treasury via the Joint Economic Committee

    Your down payment is your shock absorber.

    Think about buying a house. If you put 20 percent down and borrow the rest, you own the first slice of any loss. If the house drops 10 percent in value, you lose half your down payment, but the bank is fine. If you put only 10 percent down and the house drops 10 percent, your whole down payment is gone. One more dip and the bank starts losing money too.

    Big projects work the same way. The owners' money, called , sits on top and takes losses first. The lenders sit underneath. Van Nieuwerburgh's point about a 90 percent borrowed project is simple: a 10 percent drop in the money coming in can wipe out the owners. FACThis statement Try it.

    So the AI debt is small next to the national debt: about one dollar for every 29. The worry is not its size. It is its shape. The national debt is spread across the whole economy and backed by the government. The AI debt is packed into a few kinds of projects, it is mostly due within a few years, the projects are heavily borrowed, and some of it sits off the books of the companies that use the buildings. The question Van Nieuwerburgh asks is whether, if things go wrong, losses stay with stock investors or reach the banks holding half the loans. FACThis framing

    Why this reaches you

    Banks lend to families, small businesses and these projects from the same pile of money. If a wave of AI projects ever went bad, the bigger question would be what it does to banks' willingness to lend for car loans, mortgages and college. That has not happened, and pickier lenders now make it less likely. INFERENCE

    Both sidesExhibit 6 · The case for, and the worryProvenance: Washington Post; J.P. Morgan Asset Management; Brookings

    Is this a bubble? Smart people disagree.

    The case for calm

    • The biggest tech companies' finances "remain among the strongest" in the bond market, J.P. Morgan's asset managers wrote in August. REPORTED
    • Moody's chief credit officer, Atsi Sheth, said it is healthy that investors are getting pickier about which AI companies to fund, and that pickiness lowers the odds of a bubble. REPORTED
    • One investment strategist said investors were reassured by revenue growing across a broad set of AI customers. "There will be plenty of room for everyone to win significantly," he said. "The market is questioning how quickly that will happen." REPORTED
    • Hyperion's tenant is Meta, and Meta signed a guarantee. Lenders are not relying on a startup's promise. REPORTED

    The worry

    • The spending is bigger than any American building boom before it, about 3.6 percent of the economy a year. FACT
    • Heavily borrowed projects leave little room for surprises. At 90 percent borrowed, a 10 percent shortfall wipes out the owners. FACT
    • Loans on chips run about four to six years, shorter than the life of the buildings, and chips go out of date. FACT
    • Some commitments do not show up where most investors look, on the main company's balance sheet. FACT

    The country said no. One town did the math.

    While the money people argued about balance sheets, voters got their own say. On 3 November 2026 there were 41 local ballot measures in 10 states that could have restricted data centers, by Ballotpedia's count. Voters passed every one of them but one. REPORTEDBallotpedia via iRecruit, 5 Nov 2026 Network exit polls found 76 percent of voters would oppose a new data center in their community, and 57 percent said AI would do more to hurt than help society. REPORTEDWashington Post, 8 Nov 2026

    The one exception was Harwood, North Dakota, population 845. Applied Digital is building a $3 billion, 1.8 million square foot data center on its edge. A resident organized a ballot measure requiring independent reviews and public hearings for big industrial projects. It lost, 325 votes to 105. REPORTEDWashington Post, 8 Nov 2026

    $1.65M
    property tax the mayor expects each year · about six times what the town collected in 2024
    200+
    full time jobs the company says it will create
    325 to 105
    the vote against more oversight

    Why did Harwood go the other way? Three things the Post reported line up with the four trades in this note. The plug: the local utility is a member owned cooperative, and one resident said that once its officials walked him through the power use, he was on board, "not because I like data centers" but because the deal was good for the town. The building: the land was going to be developed either way, since Harwood and Fargo had both tried to annex it, so the mayor's argument was to take the tax revenue rather than watch a neighbor take it. The bill: a $1.65 million tax check is a very large number for a town with a couple of bars and a grain elevator. REPORTED

    The worry in Harwood was the worry in this note, stated by a resident who backed the measure. He pointed to Janesville, Wisconsin, where a closed General Motors plant left the city a cleanup bill reported at $35 million. "I compare that to what's going to happen to these data centers when they're done," he said. "Who's going to be left with the bill?" REPORTED That is the residual value question from Exhibit 4, asked by a neighbor instead of a bondholder.

    Why this reaches you

    Harwood shows the yes case is real: tax money, jobs, and a utility that answered questions. It also shows what made it work, a cooperative the residents could call and a deal where the town got paid. Most of the 40 towns that voted no did not have both. INFERENCE

    What we are not saying.

    • We are not calling a bubble or predicting a crash. Nobody knows yet whether AI revenue will grow fast enough. WATCH
    • We found nothing showing Hyperion or Meta is in financial trouble. Hyperion is an example of how these deals are built, not a warning about that building. INFERENCE
    • The $1.4 trillion is one economist's count. There is no official tally of AI building debt. WATCH
    • Anthropic's numbers come from news reports of its filing; we did not read the filing itself, and we have a stated conflict. WATCH
    • The capital stack instrument is a teaching model, not a forecast. INFERENCE
    • Harwood's vote counts, tax estimate and quotes come from one newspaper report; we have not seen the county canvass. REPORTED
