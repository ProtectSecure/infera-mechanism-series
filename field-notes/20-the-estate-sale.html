    The Estate Sale.

    When a company dies, your data is part of what it leaves behind. When a parish closes, its records move down the street. Two kinds of endings, two sets of rules.

    About 12 minutesFor high school juniors and upNo sliders · five instruments

    The short version
    1. Right now, two bankrupt companies are trying to sell what they know about their customers: the chatbot company Character.AI and Spirit Airlines. REPORTED
    2. U.S. bankruptcy law slows a data sale mainly when the company had promised not to share your data. A company that never promised much is easier to sell. INFERENCEfrom 11 U.S.C. 363(b)(1)
    3. When a Catholic parish closes, nothing is sold and nothing is deleted. The records move to the next parish and, after a century, to an archive. REPORTED

    Exhibit 3 · Read the fine print

    Which promise could stop a sale?

    Two privacy promises, 26 years apart. Tap the one that, under today's bankruptcy law, gives a court a reason to slow down a sale of your data.

    Why the promise mattersToysmart's case led Congress to act. Since 2005, the bankruptcy code says a company that promised not to transfer your personal information cannot sell it in a way that breaks that promise unless a court first hears from a consumer privacy ombudsman and finds the sale would not break other law. REPORTED11 U.S.C. 363(b)(1) and 332; ABI Journal; Arizona State Law Journal A policy that already reserves the right to transfer data in a sale does not trip that wire in the same way. INFERENCE

    Other laws can still apply. California, New York and Texas argued their genetic privacy laws barred the 23andMe sale; the judge overruled them. Not legal advice.

    Exhibit 5 · The one chart

    When customers heard, some left with their DNA.

    After 23andMe filed for bankruptcy in March 2025, customers could ask the company to delete their genetic data before any sale. About 1.9 million did, almost 15 percent of customers, according to the company's interim chief executive in testimony to the House Oversight Committee. REPORTEDSC Media

    Asked to delete · about 15 in 100Did not ask · about 85 in 100
    Do the math. The nonprofit TTAM Research Institute bought 23andMe for $305 million. California's court filings put the company's inventory at about 10 million genetic profiles. That is roughly $30 a profile. INFERENCE It is the price of the whole company, not of the data alone, and it is not what any one person's DNA is worth to them.
    Exhibit 6 · The other side

    The case for the sale.

    Why selling can protect customers

    • A buyer can keep a service running. Destroying data also destroys what customers paid for, such as ancestry results and health reports.
    • TTAM committed to follow 23andMe's privacy policy and applicable law, and customers kept the right to delete. REPORTED
    • Spirit cut passenger databases from its deal, and the ombudsman recommended approval. REPORTED
    • Money from a sale pays workers and other creditors owed by the failed company.
    • Church archives keep the family history that genealogists and families seek; digitized registers are a public good to many.

    Why critics want more

    • Ombudsmen most often rely on opt-out, which means customers must act to stay out. REPORTEDArizona State Law Journal
    • "Stripped of identifiers" is not the same as impossible to re-identify; pilots argued exactly that. REPORTED
    • The law's main trigger is a promise; a policy that promised little gets less scrutiny. INFERENCE
    • A parish file never ends, and members have no general right to delete it in most U.S. states. INFERENCEsee The Permanent Record
