Field Note № 14 · Evening Close · Friday, October 30, 2026

Why stocks fell on October 30, and what got missed.

The Nasdaq lost 2.9% in one day. The reasons trace back to oil, inflation, interest rates and the cost of building artificial intelligence. Meanwhile three bigger-than-they-looked stories barely made the screens.

How to read this note

Two colors, two threads.

Magenta marks the loud thread: what the cameras and screens were full of. Green marks the quiet thread: what mattered and went thin. Every claim carries a label. FACT means a number or filing we could read. REPORTED means a credible outlet said it and we could not check the primary source. WATCH means it may change. INFERRED is our own reasoning.

The ledger behind this note scored each event before the outcome and compared it with what actually got covered. A high score with thin coverage is a story the cameras missed. A low score with heavy coverage is a story that took the air.

EXHIBIT 1 · CATALOG R02 · MEDIUM: market close data · PROVENANCE: Yahoo Finance and investingLive snapshots, Oct 30 · LOUD THREAD · matched

The screen everyone watched.

On Friday the S&P 500 fell 1.82% to 7,580.07, the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.86% to 25,437.00, and the Dow fell 1.09% (FACT). The fear gauge, the VIX, jumped about 13% to 19.42 (FACT). Gold, usually a refuge, fell about 3.6% to roughly $3,875 even as Treasury yields dropped (FACT on prices; the cause is INFERRED, likely forced selling while stocks fell).

The trigger was earnings from the biggest technology companies. Amazon reported a record trailing outflow of free cash flow, more than $42 billion, and Alphabet had its worst day in over twenty years (REPORTED). Free cash flow is the money left over after a company pays to run and build its business. A big negative number means the company is spending more than it earns on projects such as data centers for artificial intelligence.

Why this reaches you

If you own a retirement fund or an index fund, you own these companies, and a handful of them make up a large share of the whole market. Their bad day was your bad day. Infera scored the earnings fallout 72 and it behaved like a 90, so our model under-weighted how nervous investors already were about interest rates.
EXHIBIT 2 · CATALOG R09 · MEDIUM: public-health reporting · PROVENANCE: Africa CDC and WHO figures via a daily newsletter, Oct 30 · QUIET THREAD · missed

An outbreak with a record.

In the Democratic Republic of Congo, an Ebola outbreak has passed 4,928 deaths and 10,141 cases this week, and it is now the deadliest on record, ahead of the 2014 West African outbreak (REPORTED). About 88% of cases are in one province, Ituri, but health officials warn it is spreading into North Kivu and Haut-Uele (REPORTED). The World Health Organization declared an international public health emergency in May (REPORTED). Ebola is a virus that spreads through bodily fluids and kills a large share of the people it infects.

Infera scored it 76. We estimate only about two of the six big outlets carried it on Friday, in short items (INFERRED). It lives in weekly roundups, not on front pages. Next checkpoint: the next Africa CDC weekly update (WATCH).

Why this reaches you

Outbreaks this large strain medical supply chains, aid budgets and airline screening rules, and they raise the odds that a case travels. We did not find a U.S. case in Friday's sources. The point is that a story with a record attached got a fraction of the attention of a stock chart.
EXHIBIT 3 · CATALOG R06 · MEDIUM: Federal Register public inspection · PROVENANCE: federalregister.gov, filed Oct 30 8:45 AM ET · QUIET THREAD · missed

The rule about who profits on your prescription.

The Labor Department's benefits arm filed a final rule called "Improving Transparency into Pharmacy Benefit Manager Fee Disclosure," scheduled to publish Monday, November 2 (FACT). A pharmacy benefit manager is the middleman that negotiates drug prices and decides which drugs a health plan covers. The rule would make them disclose what they are paid. Until the rule is officially published it can still be pulled back (WATCH).

Infera scored it 68. Coverage was mostly trade and health-policy press, with the major desks thin (INFERRED). Next checkpoint: Monday's Federal Register issue.

Why this reaches you

If you get health insurance through a job, part of your premium and your copay is shaped by deals between middlemen you have never seen. This rule is the first step toward seeing them.
EXHIBIT 4 · CATALOG R08b · MEDIUM: court dockets and DOJ declarations · PROVENANCE: Al Jazeera, Just Security, Reuters via others, Oct 28 to 30 · QUIET THREAD · pattern missed

Four days out, one pattern in pieces.

Each of these was reported on its own (REPORTED): the Justice Department filed a revised lawsuit in Alaska seeking to block certification if Sen. Dan Sullivan narrowly loses, with about 40 subpoenas and preservation requests. A federal judge rejected its effort to set aside roughly 20,000 California ballots, and the department plans to appeal. Washington's Secretary of State refused to set aside ballots of 400 alleged noncitizens. A department declaration says staff from the Bureau of Prisons, ATF and DEA, not the FBI or ICE, will act as poll monitors. A judge gave the department until noon Saturday, October 31, to list the states it has targeted with demands to change election administration.

The Alaska case drew full coverage from all six outlets (INFERRED). The connected pattern drew about three. We do not assert intent. We note that one agency, in one week, is pressing several states at once, and that the clock ends Tuesday, November 3. Next checkpoints: the noon Saturday deadline, the Ninth Circuit appeal.

Why this reaches you

This is about whether your ballot is counted the way your state's rules say. Seeing one lawsuit is news. Seeing five is a pattern a voter can plan around, for example by voting early and checking registration now.
EXHIBIT 5 · CATALOG R15 · MEDIUM: broadcast and live-blog coverage · PROVENANCE: CNN live blog, Reuters, newsletters, Oct 30 · LOUD THREAD · took the air

The story that took the air.

A tense exchange between the President and a Reuters reporter about his health, two canceled rallies swapped for phone-in rallies, a Halloween event, a 2028 remark by Gov. Gavin Newsom on a late-night show, and a Democratic fight in Michigan (REPORTED). Infera scored the bundle 25, because it carried no policy action and no market link. Coverage: all six outlets, by our estimate (INFERRED).

The connective tissue

One machine, two outcomes.

The stock selloff and the thin coverage are linked in two ways, and the first link is the cost of money. This chain is our INFERRED reading, not a proven sequence:

Link 1 · oilCrude near $103 a barrel on Houthi attacks on Saudi energy sites and reports the U.S. may resume strikes on Iran. U.S. gasoline averaged $4.22 on Thursday (REPORTED).
▼ pushes up prices people pay
Link 2 · inflationSeptember inflation by the Fed's preferred gauge came in hotter than expected (REPORTED).
▼ makes the Fed lean toward raising rates
Link 3 · interest ratesThe Fed held at 3.75% to 4.00% on Wednesday in a 10 to 2 vote, with both dissenters wanting a hike; futures priced about an 85% chance of a December hike (REPORTED). On Friday four Fed officials spoke, and most sounded hawkish (REPORTED).
▼ makes borrowing and waiting more expensive
Link 4 · AI spendingCompanies spending record sums on data centers, with Amazon's cash flow deeply negative, are judged harder when money costs more (INFERRED).
▼ shows up as
Result · the red screenNasdaq −2.86%. Gold −3.6%. Loudest story of the day.
▼ and the attention it consumed
Result · the quiet threadAn Ebola record, a drug-price rule and a connected election-pressure pattern all drew a fraction of the coverage (INFERRED).
The same machine produced both: when the cost of money rises, the loud thing is the price on the screen, and the quiet thing is everything with a deadline.

There is a second, smaller link. Infera's own policy board shows data centers and electricity bills as a live midterm issue, so the AI-spending story that moved markets on Friday is also on the ballot Tuesday (REPORTED). The money and the vote are the same subject.
