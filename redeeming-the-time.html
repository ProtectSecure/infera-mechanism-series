Redeeming the Time

The feed can make an hour disappear. Should the church learn the trick?

"Redeeming the time," the King James Version renders Ephesians 5:16. A feed is built to spend it. This exhibit starts by taking a little of yours, then asks what lost hours do to faith, whether the gospel should borrow the tools that took them, and where persuasion becomes manipulation of the mind. It ends with the evidence on giving the hours back.

      ROOM F · GIVING THE HOURS BACK

      Does stepping away help? A little, measurably.

      Seven studies, ordered from strongest design to weakest. Toggle the weaker designs off and see what is left.

      Evidence ladder · 7 studies
        What this showsRest from screens helps a little, and the strongest experiments agree on the direction. The effects are small: in the two largest, under a tenth of a standard deviation. One experiment estimated that self-control problems drive about 31% of social media use, and that letting people set their own future limits cuts use substantially. That is the secular case for a rule of life. FACTstudy finding INFERENCE
        BOTH SIDES, AT THEIR STRONGEST

        Should a church compete for attention with the feed's own tools?

        Yes: go where people are, with the best tools there are

        • Every era's evangelists used its dominant medium, from letters and roads to radio and satellite; the Billy Graham association says Graham preached to nearly 215 million people. REPORTEDorganization's own figure
        • A streak is a rule of life with a counter; monastic traditions have always used outside structure to form habits. Hallow lets users hide streaks and add back missed days. FACTcompany documentation
        • Hallow's founder asks "how can you get somebody to stop scrolling for half a second and let Jesus into their hearts." REPORTED
        • A Catholic writer with a 1,005-day prayer streak urges readers to pray in moments they would spend scrolling, and calls the app a habit-builder, not a teacher of prayer. REPORTED

        No: faith is a free act, and the tools are built to get around freedom

        • The faiths' own texts set the bar at freedom and time for reflection, and the research shows people cannot see their own screen time and lose control to autoplay. FACT
        • Targeting people the system judges anxious, addicted or in a struggling marriage, as one church-data firm said it could, is the exploitation of vulnerability the 2011 code rejects. REPORTEDcompany claim, as reported
        • Faith apps carry ordinary app-economy harms: a prayer app with more than 98 million downloads, by the company's count, sent location data to a broker linked to military buyers. REPORTED
        • Reach is not response. One campaign's reported figures: about 30,000 reading-plan sign-ups from roughly 100 million people exposed. REPORTEDcampaign figures as reported

        Where both sides can meet

        • A reminder that points away from the app, toward silence, a prayer time or a congregation, is different from a mechanic that keeps someone inside it.
        • The ethical tests in the traditions' own texts are consent, time to reflect, no inducement and no exploitation of need. None of them is the medium.
        • What this exhibit did not prove. We did not show that phones reduce religious practice; no study we found measured it with logged data. The Barna and Pew worship figures are surveys from 2014 and 2019.
        • Room A measures your session in this page only; it is a demonstration, not a study. The lab figures come from small samples, and the dissociation finding from a university release of a peer-reviewed paper.
        • The lawsuit claims in Room D are allegations, and the Los Angeles verdict is under appeal. Faith-app details come from company help pages, a data provider's estimates and press reports; we did not test the apps.
        • The campaign funnel and the church-data firm's claims are as reported by others. The ladder's rungs are Infera's ordering; the traditions quoted do not rank them.
        • Buddhist, Bahá'í and Hindu teachings on time and attention are not covered because we could not source them properly in time. That is a gap, not a judgment.
        FORM YOUR OWN READ

        Five questions for a week.

        1. How long were you gone in Room A, and how long did it feel?
        2. Which hours does our tradition set apart, and do our own apps and channels respect them?
        3. Do our tools point people away from the screen, or keep them on it?
        4. Would we be comfortable if every person we reached knew exactly how we reached them?
        5. When did our congregation last stop, all at once, on purpose?
